Abstract

In isotropic coordinates the Schwarzschild metric satisfies N = 2/ψ − 1 for every mass, so if the lapse is a function of the isotropic conformal factor alone, vacuum general relativity fixes that function (in areal coordinates the same argument gives a different closure, Jacobson 2007: the choice of the isotropic variable is an assumption). Imposing this closure inside matter, while keeping the Hamiltonian constraint and energy–momentum conservation of a perfect fluid, gives a phenomenological closure of the static equations in which the fluid pressure does not enter the field equations. Read with Einstein's equations, the stars are anisotropic. For uniform density, in closed form: maximum compactness 2^(1/3) − 2^(−1/3) = 0.4662 against Buchdahl's 4/9, surface redshift 2.847 against 2, and 0.4043 against 3/8 if p_c ≤ ρ is imposed. For five density profiles the maximum compactness is 3–5% above GR. At observed compactness the required central pressure is already lower than in GR (by 7% at C = 0.15), so realistic mass–radius curves differ. With the SLy and APR4 equations of state the closure raises the maximum mass by about 5% and the radius of a 1.4 solar-mass star by 0.1 km, about ten times less than present NICER errors. GR and the closure are both compatible with PSR J0437−4715; at 2.08 solar masses both give radii below the 68% interval of Salmi et al. (2024) for PSR J0740+6620. With current data the closure cannot be distinguished from general relativity.

In short

What a compact star is

A star is held together by its own gravity. The pressure of the matter inside the star pushes outwards and keeps it in equilibrium. A neutron star is a dead star, squeezed very hard. It has roughly the mass of the Sun in a ball as big as a city.

The compactness C tells how squeezed a star is. It is computed with the ratio GM/(Rc²). Here M is the mass, R the radius, G the gravitational constant and c the speed of light. The larger the mass and the smaller the radius, the larger C is.

The Buchdahl limit

In Einstein's general relativity (we write it GR) there is a famous limit. Buchdahl found it in 1959. It holds for a static star, that is, one at rest, made of a perfect fluid, with a density that does not increase going outwards. Such a star cannot be more compact than 4/9. Beyond that value the pressure at the centre would have to be infinite.

A different rule

This work studies a different rule for the inside of a static, spherical star. The rule ties two numbers together.

  • The lapse N tells the rate of clocks. Near a mass clocks run slower: N is smaller than 1.
  • The isotropic conformal factor ψ tells how much space there really is in a stretch of the map: near a mass, more rulers are needed to cover the same stretch.

The rule is:

N = 2/ψ − 1.

For a single spherical body in vacuum this relation holds exactly. It is a property of the Schwarzschild metric, that is, of Einstein's solution for the empty space around a spherical mass, written in isotropic coordinates.

Imposing it inside matter as well changes only one thing in the equations: pressure no longer acts as a source of the lapse. In GR pressure contributes to gravity, a bit like mass. Here it no longer does so for the rate of clocks.

Let us read the resulting metric with Einstein's equations. Then it looks as if there were the fluid plus an extra stress, called the anisotropic effective stress. Anisotropic means that it is not the same in all directions.

The differences from GR inside matter are small, of order p/ρ. Here p is the pressure and ρ the energy density. In ordinary stars p/ρ is tiny. This is why the differences appear only in neutron stars.

The results

  • For uniform density, that is, the same everywhere, the sequence of stars ends at a compactness 2^(1/3) − 2^(−1/3) ≈ 0.466. It is an exact formula. In GR the limit is instead 4/9 ≈ 0.444. If the density decreases outwards, the maximum is lower (see the table further on).
  • For uniform density, at compactness between 0.15 and 0.23, the pressure needed at the centre is 7–12% lower than in GR.
  • With two realistic nuclear equations of state the maximum mass rises by about 5%. The radii change by about 0.1 km. This is less than the present errors of the NICER measurements. With these two equations of state GR and the rule are compatible with the star PSR J0437−4715. And both give radii below the 68% interval of Salmi and colleagues for the star PSR J0740+6620.

The rule is an assumption, not a derivation. And it is not yet a theory: there is no action and there is no dynamics. An action is the formula from which all the equations of a theory are obtained. The dynamics tells how things change in time.

Why this rule

In vacuum, for a single body, the lapse depends only on the isotropic conformal factor: N = 2/ψ − 1. The Schwarzschild metric depends on the mass only through M/r. So the same formula holds for every mass M.

The assumption of this work is that the same formula also holds inside matter. In GR the Hamiltonian constraint gives ψ from the energy density alone. Pressure, instead, enters the equation for the lapse. Imposing N = N(ψ), that is, N as a function of ψ only, therefore removes pressure as a source of the lapse. The Hamiltonian constraint and the fluid equations stay the same.

The rule was suggested to the author by an informal picture. In this picture the lapse at a place depends only on the conformal factor at that place. It does not depend on the internal stresses of matter. For readers who want it, the picture is explained in a separate document. Nothing in the calculations depends on it.

Neutron stars are the only static objects in which pressure is comparable to the energy density. So they are the place where this rule can be put to the test.

The equations

We use isotropic coordinates and a static, spherical star. The metric is ds² = −N²dt² + ψ⁴(dr² + r²dΩ²). We use units in which G = c = 1. ρ is the total energy density measured by static observers. There are three equations.

  1. Hamiltonian constraint, as in general relativity: ∇²ψ = −2πρψ⁵. It tells how the density curves space.
  2. The rule, in place of GR's lapse equation, which is ∇²(Nψ) = 2πNψ⁵(ρ + 6p): N = 2/ψ − 1, that is, ψ + Nψ = 2.
  3. Matter: a perfect fluid that obeys the conservation of energy and momentum, ∇_μT^μν = 0. That is, p′ = −(ρ + p)N′/N, with p = 0 at the surface. In words: each layer of the star is in equilibrium.

The symbol ∇² is called the Laplacian. It measures how much a value at a point differs from the average of the nearby points.

Where the rule comes from, and what is assumed

In isotropic coordinates the Schwarzschild metric has ψ = 1 + M/2r and N = (1 − M/2r)/(1 + M/2r). So N = 2/ψ − 1 for every M. Suppose that the lapse is a function of the isotropic conformal factor alone. Then vacuum GR fixes that function in only one way.

The choice of variable matters. One could use areal coordinates. In those, the same argument gives N²g_RR = 1 (Jacobson 2007). Inside matter that relation forces p_r = −ρ and allows compactness up to 1/2. The results of this work are a property of the isotropic rule. The isotropic variable is the author's choice. It is an assumption: the spatial metric is taken to be conformally flat, with ψ as the physical variable, and the rule is imposed inside matter too. ψ is obtained by integrating the constraint over the whole star. So the rule is a pointwise relation between two fields. It is not a local equation of state.

One body only. All this holds for exact spherical symmetry. Let us try two masses: ψ = 1 + a₁/|x − x₁| + a₂/|x − x₂|. This ψ is harmonic. It is the Brill–Lindquist conformal factor for two bodies at rest at one instant. With N = 2/ψ − 1 the static metric is not a vacuum solution. Its Ricci tensor, which measures curvature, is not zero. It has a term of order a₁a₂ in an open region. This term vanishes when either of the two masses is removed.

What it implies

The fluid pressure does not enter the field equations. The metric, and hence its effective stress, is fixed by ρ alone.

Let us read the same metric with Einstein's equations. It has an anisotropic effective stress: the radial pressure p_r (outwards) differs from the tangential one p_t (along the surface), with p_t > p_r. Already in the Newtonian limit, that is, for weak gravity, the effective radial stress at the centre is half the fluid pressure. The difference between the effective stress and the fluid stress is conserved by itself and has zero energy density.

This is a phenomenological closure of the static equations, not yet a theory: there is no action and there is no dynamics. A phenomenological closure is a practical rule that completes the equations and lets us do the calculations.

Local versus global. Far from the star ψ → 1 + M_ADM/2r and N → 1 − M_Komar/r. M_ADM and M_Komar are two ways of measuring the total mass from far away. Taking the integral over all space one finds ∫∇²(ψ + Nψ) d³x = 4π(M_Komar − M_ADM). In GR the quantity inside the integral, 2πψ⁵[N(ρ + 6p) − ρ], is not zero inside matter. But its integral is zero, because the Komar and ADM masses coincide for stationary spacetimes that become flat far away (Beig 1978). The rule, instead, sets that quantity to zero point by point. In GR the balance works out only for the total; here it works out at every point.

Related theories

Other proposals also leave GR unchanged in vacuum (up to a cosmological constant) and change only the interior of matter:

  • Eddington-inspired Born–Infeld gravity (Bañados and Ferreira 2010); its neutron stars were studied by Pani, Cardoso and Delsate (2011);
  • Palatini f(R) gravity (Olmo 2011).

These theories differ from the rule of this work in two ways. First: they follow from an action, which the rule does not have. Second: their interiors depend on a coupling: κ in Born–Infeld gravity, the function f in Palatini f(R). Here instead, once the isotropic variable is chosen (an assumption, stated above), the relation between N and ψ inside matter is the one fixed by the vacuum solution. The rule has no coupling constant. The equation of state remains an input. For uniform density this gives a single value: 0.466.

Where the differences come from

In these variables ψ is sourced only by the energy density, both in GR and with the rule. This happens through the same Hamiltonian constraint. The only difference is the equation for the lapse.

In GR pressure is a source of the lapse. In covariant form this is written D²N = 4πN(ρ + 3p). In the flat-Laplacian form used above it becomes ∇²(Nψ) = 2πNψ⁵(ρ + 6p). Here instead the lapse follows from ψ, so pressure is not a source of it.

What does this mean in practice? In GR pressure adds gravitational pull. More pressure, more gravity, and even more pressure is needed. With the rule pressure is no longer a source of the lapse, and there is less gravitational pull due to pressure. Then the same star needs less pressure at the centre to stay in equilibrium. And a given equation of state supports a little more mass.

The two sequences of stars end for different reasons. In GR the sequence of uniform-density stars ends at 4/9, where the central pressure needed becomes infinite. With the rule it ends where the lapse at the centre reaches zero, ψ_c = 2, at 0.466. There too the pressure becomes infinite.

The effect is of relative order p/ρ. It is present in any matter. But in the Sun or the Earth p/ρ is tiny, and GR and the rule agree to that accuracy. Only in neutron stars does p/ρ reach tens of per cent. Only there do the two separate measurably.

Where the sequence of stars ends

Imagine building more and more compact stars, one after the other. Sooner or later the sequence ends: beyond a certain point the star cannot stay in equilibrium.

For uniform density one thing holds, both in GR and with the rule: the product (ρ + p)N is constant. So the pressure at the centre becomes infinite exactly when the lapse at the centre reaches zero. In GR pressure is a source of the lapse (ρ + 6p in the flat-Laplacian form). So pressure comes back and acts on the geometry. With the rule, instead, N is fixed by ψ, and ψ depends only on ρ. The sequence ends at ψ_c = 2. It is the edge of the range of values where N = 2/ψ − 1 ≥ 0. At ψ_c = 2 the lapse at the centre is zero: there the clocks stop.

Uniform density, with an exact formula. The constraint is the Lane–Emden equation with n = 5. Its solution is ψ = ψ_c(1 + 2πρψ_c⁴r²/3)^(−1/2). Here ψ_c is the value at the centre. Matching to the exterior gives ψ_R = ψ_c^(2/3). The compactness is C = GM/(Rc²) = 2(ψ_R − 1)/ψ_R², with R the areal radius.

  • GR's Buchdahl limit, C = 4/9, corresponds to ψ_R = 3/2 (ψ_c = 1.8371).
  • The limit ψ_c = 2 gives C_max = 2^(1/3) − 2^(−1/3) = 0.46622. It can also be said this way: the redshift of the light leaving the surface is z = 2.847 (in GR it is 2). The redshift tells how much light is stretched as it climbs out of the star's gravity. This value is below Ivanov's bounds for anisotropic stars: 3.842 or 5.211, depending on the energy condition imposed on the tangential pressure.
  • Let us require the fluid pressure to satisfy p_c ≤ ρ. Then one finds the equation y³ + 2y − 4 = 0, with y = ψ_c^(1/3). That is, C = 0.40427 (in GR 3/8). This holds for uniform density only.
  • The effective radial stress at the centre is p_r/ρ = (ψ_c − 1)/(3(2 − ψ_c)).

The link with Buchdahl's hypotheses. There is a theorem by Andréasson (2008). It holds for static, spherical GR solutions with two conditions: p_r ≥ 0 and p_r + 2p_t ≤ ρ. Then 2m/r ≤ 8/9, with no requirement on how the density varies. Here p_r ≥ 0. So to exceed 4/9 one must violate the trace condition, p_r + 2p_t ≤ ρ. This happens at the centre for ψ_c > 3/2, that is, C > 0.3615. GR's own uniform star also violates the same condition, for C > 5/18. What takes the star outside Buchdahl's hypotheses is the anisotropy. The dominant energy condition for the effective stress fails for ψ_c > 7/4 (C > 0.4289). But this is not needed to exceed 4/9.

Realistic equations of state and NICER

What an equation of state is. An equation of state tells how much pressure matter gives at a certain density. Think of a sponge and a stone. If you squeeze them, the sponge gives way at once, the stone resists. The nuclear matter of neutron stars has its own "hardness", still not well known. Scientists use several possible equations of state: SLy, APR4 and others. A "stiffer" one resists more.

How it enters the calculations. The same three equations as above are used. But with a realistic equation of state ε(p) for cold nuclear matter. Here ε is the total energy density, which above we called ρ. So:

  • ψ comes from the Hamiltonian constraint ∇²ψ = −2πεψ⁵ (ε the total energy density);
  • N = 2/ψ − 1;
  • p comes from conservation: p′ = −(ε + p)N′/N.

The central value ψ_c is fixed by requiring ψ → 1 at infinity. The mass is read from the exterior, ψ = 1 + M/2r. The areal radius is ψ(R)²R. The comparison is with GR, that is, with the TOV equations, using the same equation of state. We use the piecewise-polytrope forms of SLy and APR4 of Read and colleagues (2009), with their SLy crust. Stars are counted on the branch with dM/dp_c > 0, that is, where the mass grows with the central pressure. For the rule this static criterion is an assumption, because there is no dynamics.

  4. upwards;
  5. GR and the rule both fall in the interval of PSR J0437−4715;

Like GR, it gives radii below the interval of Salmi and colleagues for PSR J0740+6620. These data do not confirm it.

What is not claimed

  • It is not claimed that the rule holds inside matter: it is assumed.
  • It is not claimed that the isotropic variable is to be preferred over the others: this too is assumed.
  • There is no covariant or dynamical formulation. So nothing is said on radial stability (whether the star holds up under small pushes), on rotation or on the post-Newtonian constraints of a dynamical completion. (Version 2.34.2 adds two post-Newtonian readings: see further on.)
  • No bound is claimed over all density profiles or over all equations of state. (Version 2.34.2 proves a bound, 12/25, for profiles that do not increase outwards.)
  • Causality is not claimed. For uniform density the speed of sound is infinite both in GR and with this rule. In physics nothing should go faster than light.
  • It is not claimed that NICER data favour the rule over GR. With SLy and APR4 the two cannot be told apart within today's errors.

Added in version 2.34.1

Who had already done it: Buchdahl's 1964 assumption

The relation N = 2/ψ − 1 inside matter is not new.

  • Buchdahl's choice. In 1964 Buchdahl was looking for a relativistic version of the Emden polytrope of index 5. A polytrope is a simple model of a star. Buchdahl chose isotropic coordinates with ψ = 1 + f and N = (1 − f)/(1 + f) (his equation 2.2). That is exactly N = 2/ψ − 1. His reason was the same one given above: the exterior Schwarzschild metric has this form (his equation 2.3).
  • His calculation. Then he required the matter to be a perfect fluid. The isotropy of the pressure, that is, pressure equal in all directions, gives f f″ − 3f′² − f f′/r = 0 (his equation 2.7). The solution is f = (A + Br²)^(−1/2) (his equation 2.8).
  • With A = 0 one recovers the Schwarzschild exterior.
  • With A > 0 one has his n = 5 solution. It has infinite radius and finite mass.

A program of the package redoes this calculation. Version 2.34 cited Buchdahl (1959) for the 4/9 limit, but not this paper.

What this work does differently. Buchdahl requires the metric to be a solution of Einstein's equations with a perfect fluid. This leaves only his n = 5 family. Here instead the relation takes the place of the lapse equation, for any density profile:

  • the matter is still a perfect fluid, with the pressure following from conservation;
  • the stars have a finite radius;
  • what is dropped is the isotropy of the effective stress, the one obtained by reading the metric with Einstein's equations: that effective stress is anisotropic.

The exact maximum 2^(1/3) − 2^(−1/3) for uniform density, the five profiles and the realistic equations of state are consequences of this second choice.

Other prior work. It was found by a novelty check made on 7 October 2026:

  • the metric of Buchdahl's solution in the form ψ = 1 + f is the entry "Buch2" of the catalogue of Delgaty and Lake (1998);
  • the conformal factor of the uniform-density star is the n = 5 Lane–Emden solution of the Hamiltonian constraint. It was studied as initial data, without a lapse, by Baumgarte, Ó Murchadha and Pfeiffer (2007);
  • Puttasiddappa, Bretón and Pérez Bergliaffa (2026) build uniform-density anisotropic stars, also beyond the Buchdahl limit. In their stars the anisotropy is proportional to ρ k^μ∇_μP_r with a constant coefficient. The stars of this work are not in that family: the corresponding ratio changes along the radius.

Causal equations of state and the turning point

An equation of state is causal if the speed of sound in matter does not exceed the speed of light. As a formula: dp/dε ≤ 1. The predictions of this part were registered before the calculation.

  • Equation of state used: p = c_s²(ε − ε_s). Here c_s² is the square of the speed of sound. c_s² = 1 is the stiffest of the causal ones: sound travels at the speed of light.
  • Stability: assessed only by the turning point, that is, by the maximum of the mass along the sequence. For barotropic stars in GR this criterion is exact. The rule has no dynamics, so for it the criterion is only an indicator.

The binding energy of neutron stars

The binding energy tells how much energy is released when the star forms. Think of a brick wall: you weigh all the loose bricks, then the finished wall. For a real wall the difference cannot be seen. In a neutron star, instead, the whole star weighs less than its matter counted piece by piece. The difference, BE = M_B − M, is the binding energy. M_B is the baryonic mass, that is, the mass of all the matter counted piece by piece. M is the gravitational mass, the one measured from outside.

The predictions of this part were registered before the calculation. A check program was written after the calculation and is not registered.

  • Equations of state. The 17 piecewise polytropes of Read and colleagues (2009) are used. Their parameters had been typed from memory. For this reason they were checked against the published maximum masses: all 17 agree within 1.1 %. (Corrected in 2.34.2, correction 2: for ALF2 the reference value was wrong, and the admitted equations of state are 16, not 17.)

Results.

  • At fixed mass the binding energy is nearly the same in the two theories.
  • Between 1.25 and 1.6 M☉ the rule equals GR within 0.1 %. (Corrected in 2.34.2, correction 1: it becomes "lower by less than 0.2 %".)
  • At the GR maximum mass it is lower by 0.4–0.75 %, even though the radius there grows by 0.61–1.18 km. (Corrected in 2.34.2, correction 1: 0.43–0.80 % and 0.74–1.36 km.)
  • A likely reason is that both theories have the same Hamiltonian constraint. It is the constraint that fixes the geometry of space from the energy density. This is a reading of the numbers, not a proof.
  • The quasi-universal relation BE/M(β) does not distinguish the rule. Here β is the compactness. The mean offset is +2.4 %, while the scatter of GR is 4.3 %.
  • The pulsar PSR J0737−3039B (Podsiadlowski and colleagues 2005) selects the same five equations of state in the two theories.

Two predictions did not hold. Here is the registered text, translated from Italian, and then the outcome.

  • E2 (a check in GR): "the fit BE/M = d₁β + d₂β² over all stable stars of the admitted equations of state has d₁ between 0.55 and 0.70 and a relative (rms) scatter below 5 %". Outcome: d₁ = 0.480 and d₂ = 0.634, with scatter 4.34 % (505 stars). The scatter holds, d₁ does not.

Outcome: the differences lie between −0.10 % and +0.02 %. (Corrected in 2.34.2, correction 1: with the exact mass the rule is lower by 0.02–0.05 %; E3 still does not hold.)

A caveat on the absolute values. The registered range for d₁ came from published fits (Lattimer and Prakash 2001; Breu and Rezzolla 2016). So the outcome of E2 means that our GR binding energies are lower than those fits. A possible cause is the unit of baryon mass. It is not isolated here. This does not affect the comparison between the two theories, which use the same definition. It does affect, instead, which equations of state are compatible with J0737−3039B.

Three registered predictions out of five held.

What changes in what is claimed (version 2.34.1)

  • The relation is Buchdahl's. This work proposes to use it as a rule for any density, and studies its consequences.
  • Causal equations of state. Using the turning point as an indicator, the rule reaches C = 0.366 at maximum mass. This is below the cited benchmarks: 0.389 (causal and radially stable configurations) and 0.462 (causal configurations). The radial stability of the rule has not been established.

  • More precise mass and radius measurements (radii to about 0.1 km) or an independently constrained equation of state could help. Tidal deformability and the oscillations of the star would also be useful. But the rule has no dynamics to compute them.

The new results

The effective stress with exact formulas. Let us read with Einstein's equations any configuration of the rule, with any density profile. The symbolic calculation gives:

  • ρ_eff = ρ;
  • the trace S = p_r + 2p_t = ρ(ψ − 1)/(2 − ψ);
  • p_r = −ψ′[(rψ)′ − 1]/[2πrψ⁵(2 − ψ)] ≥ 0.

The trace condition, p_r + 2p_t ≤ ρ, holds exactly where ψ ≤ 3/2.

A bound for densities that do not increase. Every configuration of the rule with a density that does not increase outwards has C < 12/25 = 0.48. The proof has three steps:

  1. q = ρψ⁵ does not increase; so m̃(r)/r³, with m̃ = −r²ψ′, does not increase;
  2. it follows that ψ_c − ψ_R ≥ (ψ_R − 1)/2;
  3. the lapse at the centre must be positive, ψ_c < 2; this gives ψ_R < 5/3.

Some notes:

  • Initial data. The bound uses only the Hamiltonian constraint and ψ_c < 2.
  • Without the non-increase condition thin shells approach C = 1/2, that is, 2M/R → 1.
  • Equation of state. For stars built from a barotropic equation of state the density automatically does not increase.
  • Numerical checks. No step profile exceeds the uniform value 2^(1/3) − 2^(−1/3) = 0.46622. This value remains a conjecture for the true maximum:
  • out of 120 non-increasing step profiles taken at random, the largest is 0.4662163;
  • a local optimisation over two-step profiles finds the uniform one again. The program was written independently by an AI assistant acting as a referee.

The interior with the post-Newtonian expansion. The post-Newtonian expansion is a way of computing, step by step, the corrections to Newton's gravity. To post-Newtonian order the spatial metric is the same in the two theories. The lapse, instead, gives:

  • −g₀₀ = 1 − 2U + 2U² − 4Φ₂ − 6Φ₄ in GR;
  • −g₀₀ = 1 − 2U + 2U² − 5Φ₂ with the rule.

Here U, Φ₂, Φ₄ are "potentials", that is, numbers that depend on how matter is distributed. The second line can be read in two ways.

  • Read within GR. The metric is that of GR with an effective stress whose trace is S = ρU/2 to leading order. All the post-Newtonian parameters are those of GR. The rule becomes a statement about matter. But that matter is nonlocal: through ψ, the stress at a point depends on the density of the whole configuration, inside and outside r, and on the normalization of ψ at infinity. This is a stronger nonlocality than in the equations of state of Hernández and Núñez (2004), which depend only on the matter enclosed within r.
  • With p as the physical pressure. Then the rule acts as a modification of gravity inside matter. Some parameters are found:
  • ξ = 0 is forced. Outside a sphere, Φ_W has a term in 1/r⁴ that no other potential cancels, and the exterior of the rule is exactly Schwarzschild. There is a Monte Carlo check, that is, one with random numbers.
  • Matching the remaining coefficients gives β = γ = 1, ζ₃ = 0, ζ₂ = 1/2 and ζ₄ = −1.
  • Every theory derived from an invariant action has α₃ = ζᵢ = 0 (Lee, Lightman and Ni 1974).
  • Binary pulsars bound |α₃ + ζ₂| < 4×10⁻⁵, and hence ζ₂ (Will 2014). In strong field |ζ̂₂| < 1.3×10⁻⁵ (Freire and Wex 2024).
  • So, in this reading, the rule is not the static limit of any metric theory derived from an action, nor of any metric theory compatible with these bounds. It is a reading that falls: the rule taken as modified gravity.
  • Moreover, by an argument of Will (1976, 2014), in any reasonable theory 6ζ₄ = 3α₃ + 2ζ₁ − 3ζ₃. Then ζ₄ = −1 with ζ₃ = 0 would require 3α₃ + 2ζ₁ = −6, while |α₃| < 4×10⁻²⁰.

Causal stars: the effective stress.

  • Slopes. In the maximum-mass rule star with c_s² = 1, the slopes dp_r/dε and dp_t/dε of the effective stress do not exceed 0.922 and 0.917. They are not characteristic speeds, that is, the speeds at which waves would travel in the medium: the effective medium has no prescribed response to perturbations.
  • Dominant energy condition. It holds in this star.
  • The trace at the centre of the maximum-mass stars. Let us compare the rule's S/ε with GR's 3p_c/ε_c:
  • c_s² = 1: 1.52 against 2.01;
  • c_s² = 1/2: 0.90 against 1.13;
  • c_s² = 1/3: 0.65 against 0.79;
  • SLy: 1.20 against 1.62;
  • APR4: 1.52 against 2.11.

Glossary

  • Action. The formula from which all the equations of a theory are obtained. The rule of this work does not have one.
  • Anisotropic. Different depending on the direction. An anisotropic stress pushes differently outwards and along the surface.
  • Binding energy. The difference between the mass of the matter counted piece by piece and the mass of the whole star.
  • Buchdahl limit. In GR, a perfect-fluid star with non-increasing density cannot exceed C = 4/9.
  • Causal. An equation of state is causal if sound in matter does not go faster than light: dp/dε ≤ 1.
  • Closure, rule. The relation N = 2/ψ − 1 imposed inside matter as well, in place of GR's lapse equation.
  • Compactness C. How squeezed a star is: C = GM/(Rc²).
  • Conformal factor ψ. The number that tells how much real space there is in a stretch of the map, at a place, compared with a place very far away: near a mass more rulers are needed for the same stretch.
  • Equation of state. The rule that tells how much pressure matter gives at a certain density, that is, how "hard" it is.
  • General relativity (GR). Einstein's theory of gravity.
  • Lapse N. The number that tells how slowly a clock runs at a place, compared with a distant clock.
  • M☉. The mass of the Sun, used as a unit.
  • Neutron star. A dead star, squeezed very hard: about the mass of the Sun in a ball as big as a city.
  • NICER. An instrument on the International Space Station that measures the mass and radius of pulsars.
  • Post-Newtonian. The way of computing, step by step, the corrections to Newton's gravity.
  • Pulsar (PSR). A rotating neutron star that sends out regular pulses.
  • Turning point. The maximum of the mass along a sequence of stars. In GR, beyond that point the stars are not stable; for the rule, which has no dynamics, it is only an indicator.
