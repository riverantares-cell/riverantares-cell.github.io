Ugo Lanciano · Independent researcher, Locri, Italy · ORCID 0009-0008-7618-9635

Mathematical Materiality (MM) in brief

Already read it? Go straight to the list of works ↓

Why are the laws of physics the same everywhere and at every instant, even though nothing forces matter to obey them? MM proposes an answer: the laws are not rules imposed on matter from the outside; rather, matter itself is made of mathematical relations. And mathematics cannot change: a relation binds two distinct terms everywhere and always. In logic, nothing exists on its own: to exist means to be in relation to another logical entity, and outside this web of relations nothing can exist, not even other universes.

This reading is already compared with experimental measurements at two points. First, the ratio of dark to ordinary matter is read as 3√3 = 5.196; when left free in the fits, Planck and DESI DR2 data give 5.21–5.22 (as registered in record 2.44, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23329080). Second, the acceleration scale of galaxies is read as a₀ = cH₀/6, the same scale that appears in Verlinde's emergent gravity: the computed value is 1.09–1.18 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s² against the measured value of 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s² (record 2.46.1, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23506655). Both readings were formulated with the measurements already known; the tests to check them against future data are registered in the same records.

MM also changes the picture of the world. Reality is not made of separate objects, each complete in itself, in empty space: it is a material structure whose nature is a logical description. The observer does not look at it from outside: it is inside it, and always finds the logical mirror of its own position in front of itself. The objects it sees are, necessarily, diffractions of its point of view: the parts into which its description divides, like a unit into fractions. But each object in turn exists in its own logical mirror, exactly like its observer. This is why things do not exist only when someone looks at them, and yet none exists on its own: every property of a thing is a link with its observer. For MM, this is the only rational answer to the opening question: if everything is made of the relations that the laws describe, nothing can escape the laws and no one has to enforce them.

For this reason the universe behaves like a simulation without really being one. It follows exact rules, made of counts, everywhere and at every instant, but no machine runs them and no programmer wrote them: a structure of mathematical relations does not need to be computed in order to exist. Tegmark (2008) had already noted that such a simulation need not be run, while Vazza (2025) estimated that running it physically on a machine would require resources that the very laws of the universe do not allow.

In the first logical nexus between space and time — which relativity describes together as a single spacetime — there is a non-local phase, made only of a pure logical distinction: space is not time. In this phase, between space and time there is no relation of quantitative calculation, only the logical entity they have in common, which is needed to make them exist with respect to each other: we shall call this entity their greatest common divisor (GCD), just as the numbers 3 and 5 have only 1 in common. "Non-local" here does not mean that something is confined far away or acts at a distance: it means that the non-local phase is in no place and at no instant, while being present in every phase of reality, in every place and at every instant (MM 2.72). This non-local phase manifests itself in entanglement: two distant systems, without anything physically travelling from one to the other, remain correlated more strongly than any explanation based on separate properties and on the absence of faster-than-light influences allows: this is the violation of Bell's inequalities.

The local world arises when a relation is counted through this GCD: its multiples and its fractions form a matrix. Seen by an observer inside the system like us, the multiples, which follow one another in a chain that never reverses, appear as time; the fractions, which stand side by side, appear as space. When that same multiple is directly contained in the matrix of the point of observation, it turns into mass — that is, into concrete matter as we usually understand it. There is therefore a close link between time and mass, which physics also recognizes: every mass has its own rhythm, its Compton frequency mc²/h (MM 2.64.1). The observed objects, while being logically autonomous entities in their local relations, enter into relation with the observer through the logical diffraction of the observer's matrix.

This reading also gives a reason for the limit of the speed of light, which in Einstein's relativity is instead taken as a starting principle. The speed of light is the fixed exchange rate between the multiples and the fractions of the same GCD, meaning how much space a single step of time is worth: exceeding it would mean, for some observer, covering space without counting time, and thus leaving the local world. Logic always starts from a logical relative, and this exchange rate has nothing absolute in itself either: it is a necessary relative which, inside the system, becomes an absolute hypothesis, the same for everyone and impossible to exceed. Outside the system, by contrast, it has no justification at all, because outside there is only non-existence: justifying something requires another term to relate it to, and out there there is none. Asking for a reason from outside is like asking what lies north of the North Pole.

The same theoretical framework that reads space, time, mass and the observed objects also admits quantum mechanics. The non-local phase and the mirror-like logical reciprocity between the two ends of the nexus give a reason for the probabilistic behaviour of matter. When the mass of the observed object is large enough compared with that of the point of observation, it fixes the object in the matrix of the latter: for this reason we see it in one precise place and at one precise instant. When instead this ratio falls below a certain threshold, the principal GCD (that of the point of observation) and the GCD of the diffraction that generates the object are forced to become equal, and they reflect each other as in a mirror. At that point the object no longer occupies a single point of the spacetime generated by the observer, but spreads over several possible positions, and we observe it as probability. It is like having to describe the number 10 using only the number 1: the only way is to distribute the 1 into ten copies, one for each unit of the 10. The probability here is not that of a die: these copies add up like waves and can also cancel each other out.

This adding up and cancelling out is called interference. It can be seen in the double-slit experiment: where the waves arrive in step the screen lights up, and where they arrive out of step they cancel each other and leave dark stripes, the "dark fringes". This is what separates quantum probability from the probability of someone who simply does not know: the possibilities of a hidden die only add up, they never cancel. In quantum mechanics the probability of finding the object at a point is given by the Born rule: it is the squared modulus of the amplitude of the wave at that point, and the larger the amplitude there, the more likely it is to find the object there. In MM this rule follows from counting the copies, under three stated hypotheses (MM 2.48.2). And the cancellations appear only if the copies carry a wave phase, that is, if, like waves, they can be at a crest or a trough and so cancel each other out. In MM, if what is observed at each place follows the Born rule, entanglement too has a ceiling, the same as in quantum mechanics. The correlations between two distant systems exceed those that any classical explanation allows, but not by as much as one likes: in the quantity measured in these experiments (CHSH), classical explanations reach at most 2, quantum mechanics 2√2, about 2.83 (Tsirelson's bound). Measurements so far are compatible with this ceiling (MM 2.48.2).

It remains to be understood how far all this goes. Today we know that even objects made of thousands of atoms spread over several positions, and no experiment has yet found a mass beyond which an object stops doing so and returns to a single place. That the transition depends on mass is an idea close to that of the physicists Roger Penrose and Lajos Diósi, for whom, however, the time within which an object stops being in several positions depends only on the object itself, on its mass and on how that mass is distributed. In MM, by contrast, probabilistic behaviour arises from a ratio. The threshold is the point at which the two GCDs, the principal one and that of the local diffraction, become equal: it is not a fixed value of mass but a ratio between the mass of the observed object and that of the point of observation, that is, of the physical system observing it, which changes from one experiment to another. For this reason MM indirectly gives a reason why experiments find no definite mass boundary: there is no single value to find, and in no experiment so far has the object come close to the mass of its own point of observation. The predictions, to be tested by experiments by 2035, are registered in MM 2.66.1.
