The question

What physics usually means

In physics the word "non-locality" mostly names one fact: the correlations of entanglement.

Two results are correlated when they agree more often than chance would. An example with everyday objects. Take a pair of gloves. Put one glove in a box and the other in another box, without looking. Send the two boxes to two distant cities. Whoever opens the first box and finds the right glove knows at once that the left one is in the other. No message has gone from one box to the other. The information was in the boxes from the start.

In 1964 the physicist John Bell proved a theorem on the correlations between two distant systems. It is Bell's theorem. It starts from three assumptions:

  • each system carries its own properties with it, like the gloves in the boxes;
  • no influence travels faster than light;
  • the choices of the measurements are independent of the state of the source, that is, of what produces the two systems.

The theorem shows that these three assumptions, together, put a limit on the correlations. It is the Bell limit. The theorem is used in this form also in deposit 2.68.

Experiments exceed that limit. And physics treats non-locality as a property of entangled systems, that is, systems linked by entanglement.

The rest of physics is local

Elsewhere physics is local: it describes each thing in a place and at an instant. Physics accounts for some very general facts with separate assumptions, one for each fact:

  • the laws are the same everywhere. Physics accounts for this with the homogeneity of space and time: the assumption that no place and no instant is special;
  • all electrons are identical. Physics accounts for this with a field for each species of particle (we will see what a field is in §3.3);
  • two observers see the same object. Physics accounts for this with the redundancy of the copies in the environment, that is, with the many traces of the object that spread around (§3.4).

MM starts from the opposite end

MM starts from the other end. Its foundation is the non-local phase. The local world is the same reality read from a point.

If so, non-locality cannot appear only in entanglement. It must be at work wherever something is in a place.

1. What "non-local" means in MM

Not at a distance, but nowhere

In MM "non-local" means not located in space and time. It does not mean "at a distance". Nor does it mean "faster than light".

Space and time are not the container of the relation: they are not a box that holds it. They are the way it is read.

Two phases, but not two stages

The framework distinguishes two phases: a non-local phase and a local phase. They are not two stages, one before and one after. Nor are they two floors of a building, one above the other.

  • The non-local phase has no time. So it comes before nothing.
  • It has no place. So it is not in a location from which a distance separates us.
  • It is present in every thing, in every place and at every instant.

The local phase is the same reality counted from a point.

A changed way of speaking. In earlier deposits the same thing is also called "level" or "underlying step". Here it is said without the image of floors: present everywhere. So does the entry page of the framework, that is, the deposit that presents MM.

The nexus between space and time

In the non-local phase there is only a logical distinction: space is not time.

A reminder of arithmetic is needed. The greatest common divisor (GCD) of two whole numbers is the largest number that divides both of them. For 3 and 5 the GCD is 1.

Space and time, the two distinct terms, have a single element in common. MM calls it their greatest common divisor, the GCD. It is as with 3 and 5, which have only 1 in common.

Between 3 and 5 there is no intermediary. Nothing travels from one to the other. The relation holds entirely and at once.

Counting gives rise to the local world

The relation can be counted through its GCD. Then the multiples of the GCD arise (twice, three times, and so on) and its fractions (one half, one third, and so on). Multiples and fractions together form a matrix. Here the word does not mean a table from algebra: it means the set of the multiples and the fractions. The matrix is the local world.

An observer is inside the matrix. For the observer the multiples appear as time and the fractions as space.

Two postulates

A postulate is a starting statement: it is not proved, it is assessed by its consequences. Two postulates of the framework, from the entry page (record 22667516), do the work in everything that follows.

  • Existing is reciprocal: an entity exists for another only to the extent of what they have in common. An entity is any thing at all.
  • Copies have no distance: three copies are the same unit counted three times, and between one copy and another there is no distance. Think of 3: it is 1 counted three times. The three copies of 1 are the same unit.

Hence the sentence that sums up the framework: the local is the non-local counted. Every distance is made of copies between which there is no distance.

The four senses of the word

In philosophy of physics "non-local" has at least four senses. 2.68 separates them as follows.

  • Non-location. The relation is not in a place or at an instant. In MM: it is the sense of MM.
  • Non-separability (Howard 1985; Teller 1986). Two systems do not have separate states of their own. The state of a system is its complete description in physics. In MM: it is a consequence. Two copies of the same unit are not two things.
  • Bell non-locality (Bell 1964). These are correlations that no explanation reproduces, if the explanation uses only properties of their own, influences no faster than light and independent measurements. In MM: it is a datum. MM reads it with non-separability and with the loop (§3.1).
  • Action at a distance. An influence that goes from here to there faster than light. In MM: it is excluded.

What is not claimed. The non-local phase transmits no signals and no influences. Experiments have events (what happens), settings (the choices on how to measure) and records (the written results). All this is in spacetime as always (2.48, §3). Spacetime is space and time taken together, as in relativity.

3.1 The correlations of entanglement and their ceiling (ways A and C)

First, a few words.

  • A photon is a particle of light.
  • Spin is a property of particles that is measured along a chosen direction. For an electron the measurement always gives one of two results, called up and down. Spin is not a little ball that spins.
  • In Bell experiments a quantity called S is measured. S puts together the correlations obtained with two different settings for each side. The Bell limit, for S, is 2.
  • The number after the ± sign is the uncertainty of the measurement. It says how far, with good probability, the true value can be from the value given.
  • A loophole is a way in which an ordinary explanation could still work. For example: the detectors lose too many particles; or the two sides are close enough to exchange a signal during the measurement. The best experiments close the main loopholes, that is, they exclude them.

What is observed. Correlations beyond the Bell limit are measured, with the main loopholes closed:

  • two electrons in laboratories 1.3 km apart (Hensen et al. 2015, S = 2.42 ± 0.20);
  • atoms 398 m apart (Rosenfeld et al. 2017, S = 2.221 ± 0.033).

Other experiments also go beyond the limit:

  • photons sent from a satellite to stations 1,203 km apart (Yin et al. 2017, S = 2.37 ± 0.09);
  • tests in which the settings are chosen by the light of stars and quasars (Handsteiner et al. 2017; Rauch et al. 2018). A quasar is the very bright centre of a very distant galaxy.

In these three experiments (Yin, Handsteiner, Rauch) a fair sampling of the detectors is assumed. It means that the particles detected are an honest sample of all those produced.

The correlations cannot be used to send messages. And they do not exceed a second, higher limit: the Tsirelson bound. It is 2√2, that is, 2 times the square root of 2. The experiment of Poh et al. (2015) obtained 2.82759 ± 0.00051. The central value is 0.00084 below 2√2 = 2.82843: very close to the ceiling, but below.

In 2022 the Nobel Prize in physics went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger for experiments with entangled photons.

Known physics. Quantum mechanics is the theory that describes atoms, electrons and light. It predicts these correlations with the entangled state. The ceiling comes from a theorem: Tsirelson's theorem (1980).

The interpretations of quantum mechanics are different ways of saying what happens beneath the calculations, which stay the same. The interpretations differ on what produces the correlations.

What the non-local phase does. For MM the two ends are not two systems with properties of their own. They are two copies of the same nexus (way A).

Let us go back to Bell's three assumptions, in the form used here. MM drops the first one at the root: separability, that is, the idea that each system carries its own properties with it. For MM this is a premise of the framework, not a result of the theorem.

The ceiling, instead, is set by the local reading of each end (way C).

Where the link comes from. From deposits 2.48 and 2.68 (§1 and §5), and from the overview (ch. 4). The overview is an overall view of MM.

What rests on calculations (MM 2.48 and 2.48.1).

  • The ceiling. Suppose that each end is read with the Born geometry, that is, with the rules of quantum mechanics for probabilities (the Born rule, §3.6). Then no correlation exceeds 2√2. It is a theorem, already in the scientific literature.
  • A common record. Suppose instead that the two ends carry only a common record, written at departure, like the gloves in the boxes. Then the correlations would stay below 2. But experiments exceed 2.
  • The singlet. The singlet is an entangled state of two particles with spin. If the spin of the two particles is measured along the same direction, the results are always opposite. Along different directions, how often they are opposite depends on the angle between the directions: it is the angular law. The calculation uses projective measurements (the ordinary measurements of quantum mechanics) and coplanar ones (with all the directions in the same plane). And it assumes the angular law.

With these conditions the correlations of the singlet are reproduced in the loop model, with every link inside the light cones. The light cone of an event includes all the points of spacetime linked to that event by signals at the speed of light or slower. In the model the correlations pass through the common past of the two readings. Nothing goes directly from one end to the other.

The loop is the closure of a history between preparation and outcome, in which the future contributes to choosing the past. Close ideas are those of Aharonov, Bergmann and Lebowitz (1964), of Cramer (1986), of Argaman (2010) and of Wharton and Argaman (2020).

The price is Bell's third assumption. The choices of measurement are not independent of the state, because the future enters its preparation.

What remains open.

  • Non-separability alone does not give the numbers of the correlations. The Born reading at the two ends gives them.
  • Experiments exclude Bell's three assumptions taken together. They do not choose MM among the interpretations.
  • Relativistic causality is the rule of relativity by which no influence leaves the light cones. Whether the model respects it still has to be argued separately (2.48, §5).
  • Whether the non-local phase and the loop are the same fact seen from two sides is a reading of the AI (2.68, §5).

3.2 The same laws everywhere and always (way A)

What is observed. No difference has ever been measured between the laws in different places and epochs.

A precise check concerns one constant: the fine-structure constant, written α (alpha). It is a number without units of measurement. It fixes the strength of the electric and magnetic forces between charged particles.

  • Far away in space and time. The spectrum of a light is the light divided by colours. Atoms leave lines in it at precise colours, and their position depends also on α. The number z says how much the light of a distant object has been stretched by the expansion of the universe: the larger z is, the longer ago the light set out. In the spectrum of a quasar at z = 1.15, α varies by no more than a few parts per million (Murphy et al. 2022). The result is Δα/α = 1.3 ± 1.3 ± 0.4 × 10⁻⁶. Δα/α is the change of α divided by α itself; the result has two uncertainties; 10⁻⁶ means one millionth.
  • Today, in the laboratory. In today's atomic clocks α varies by no more than about 10⁻¹⁸ per year, that is, a billionth of a billionth (Lange et al. 2021).

Known physics. For physics the universality of the laws, that is, the fact that they are the same everywhere and always, is a starting assumption.

There is a famous theorem, Noether's theorem. It ties symmetries to the quantities that are conserved, that is, that stay constant:

  • if the laws do not change when one shifts in time, energy is conserved;
  • if the laws do not change when one shifts in space, momentum (mass times velocity) is conserved.

A shift of this kind is called a translation. Not changing is called invariance. The theorem ties symmetries to conservation laws. But it does not explain why the symmetries hold.

Physics assumes universality and checks it, without giving it a reason.

What the non-local phase does. Every "here" and every "now" is a copy of the same nexus. And between two copies of the same unit there is no distance.

Two places cannot have different laws for the same reason that two copies of 1 are not two different units.

The universality of the laws is non-locality seen from inside the local.

The laws do not need a guardian to make them respected. 3 and 5 do not "obey" their GCD: they are that fact.

Where the link comes from. From deposits 2.68 and 2.68.1 (§7) and from the overview (ch. 6).

What rests on calculations. No calculation: it is an argument, that is, a line of reasoning. It concerns the laws. The single values come from measurement: MM calls them history.

What remains open. Suppose that a different law were measured in a distant region. Then this reading would lose its support.

3.3 Identical particles (way A)

Proposal of the AI, not contained in earlier deposits; the author accepted it on 28 October 2026.

What is observed. All electrons have the same mass and the same charge. And they are indistinguishable in the strict sense: if two of them are exchanged, no prediction changes. An example chosen by us, for comparison: two identical billiard balls can always be told apart, for example by following where each one goes. Two electrons cannot.

Electrons belong to a family of particles, the fermions. For fermions the state changes sign under exchange: in the mathematical description of the pair a minus appears. Which sign holds for which species of particle is fixed by a theorem, the spin-statistics theorem.

Known physics. Field theory is the language of particle physics. A field is a quantity present at every point of space. In this theory every electron is an excitation of the same field, that is, a small packet of energy of that field. Why there is a field for each species of particle, the theory does not ask.

The physicist Richard Feynman recounts an episode in his Nobel lecture (1965). The physicist John Wheeler told him on the telephone that all electrons have the same charge and the same mass "because they are all the same electron".

In philosophy it is debated whether two identical particles are two objects. Saunders (2003) argues that they are, in a weak sense.

What the non-local phase does. Two electrons are two copies of the same unit, counted twice. They are indistinguishable because between the copies there is nothing that makes them different.

The reading is close to that of field theory: the common field is the common nexus.

Where the link comes from. It is a proposal of the AI.

What rests on calculations. No calculation. The step from the copies to the sign of exchange has not been made.

What remains open. It remains open why different copies give different species of particles. For example the electron and the muon, a particle with the same charge as the electron but heavier. Without this step the argument would prove too much. An argument proves too much when, if it were right, it would also give conclusions that the facts contradict.

3.4 The objects that two observers see alike (ways A and B)

What is observed. Two people look at the same chair. The light that reaches their eyes is made of different photons. Yet they agree on where the chair is.

In laboratories a piece of information becomes objective, that is, the same for everyone, in a precise way. Many copies of the information spread into the environment. Whoever reads a small part of them finds the same value. It is quantum Darwinism (Zurek 2009). The name recalls Darwin and natural selection.

Known physics. Quantum Darwinism accounts for the agreement with the redundancy of the copies: there are many more of them than needed.

Then there is Wigner's friend, a thought experiment. An observer, the friend, makes a measurement inside a closed laboratory. A second observer, outside, treats the whole laboratory, friend included, as a quantum system.

The theorems on Wigner's friend (Bong et al. 2020) show one thing. Suppose that quantum mechanics holds also for an observer controllable as a quantum system. Then three things cannot hold together:

  • the absoluteness of outcomes: every measurement has a single result, which holds for everyone;
  • locality: no influence faster than light;
  • the absence of superdeterminism: the choices of measurement are not tied in advance to the state being measured.

What the non-local phase does. The record of an observer is what it has collected. The two records of the two observers are two counts of the same non-local relation, made from two different points. Their common part is the trace of that relation (way A).

The observed object arises as a non-local transposition and projection of the observer's matrix. These are words of the framework. The observer projects its own matrix and discards the private part, that is, the part that only it has. In this way it isolates the trace of the relation. And that trace has no distance between its copies.

It is the same idea with which the framework reads entanglement. The two distant measurements exchange nothing. They are the two ends of a single relation.

And there is a prohibition: two observers who have enough of the object cannot disagree.

Then the hardest side. What has no non-local link with us casts no shadow in our record. And for us it does not exist (way B).

A separate case is a laboratory that lets no copies out. For this case MM gives up the absoluteness of the outcome (2.48, §5).

Where the link comes from. From the deposits (2.62.1, §2.2 and §2.5) and from the author's premises of 12, 13 and 23 October.

What rests on calculations (MM 2.62 and 2.62.1). The calculations use a model of quantum Darwinism. There the common object is the Gács–Körner common part of the two records. It is a mathematical measure of the information that two records have in common exactly: each of the two can obtain it alone, without talking to the other.

The bit is the unit of information: the information of a yes-or-no answer.

  • With a tolerance of 10⁻⁶, that is, accepting a deviation of one millionth, 23 copies per observer are enough. Then the common part is almost the whole object: about 1 bit, almost all of the question "where is the chair?".
  • The exact common part, without tolerance, is zero until the records are compared.
  • With an object that can have more values, the common part grows in steps, that is, in jumps.

What remains open.

  • The calculations test the common part, not the non-local phase.
  • Identifying the copies of the framework with scattered photons, that is, photons that bounced off the object, is a choice of the AI.
  • Agreement between the readings is already guaranteed by standard quantum mechanics. MM recovers it, but does not predict it in its own right.

3.5 The boundary of the local is a count (ways A and C)

First, a few words. An interferometer is an apparatus that splits a wave over two paths and then joins them again. Where the two parts reinforce or cancel each other, light and dark stripes appear: the interference fringes. In the quantum world atoms and molecules also give fringes. The wavelength is the distance between two successive crests of a wave.

What is observed.

  • A single photon is enough. In an atom interferometer the two paths can be about half a wavelength of light apart. Then a single scattered photon, that is, one that bounced off the atom, erases the fringes. At larger distances the fringes come back in part, at intervals (Kokorowski et al. 2001).
  • Heat is enough. Fullerenes are carbon molecules shaped like a ball. Heated fullerenes lose their fringes: in the measurements they go from 47 % to 29 %, to 7 %, to 0 % (Hackermüller et al. 2004). The cause is the thermal photons that fly away, that is, the light that every hot body emits.
  • The copies carried away count even if nobody collects them.

Known physics. Known physics calls this phenomenon decoherence. It is the passage to classical behaviour, that of everyday objects. It depends on two things:

  • how many copies the environment carries away;
  • the separation between the paths compared with the wavelength.

It does not depend on the distance from whoever records.

What the non-local phase does. If the local is the non-local counted, its boundary cannot be a distance from the observer. It is a number of copies.

Let us take an ordinary record: a surface of 1 cm² at 300 K. The kelvin (K) is the unit of absolute temperature; 300 K is about the temperature of a room.

  • In one microsecond, that is, a millionth of a second, this record leaves about 4 × 10¹² copies of itself: four thousand billion.
  • For a complete reading a few tens are enough: 21 for 99 % of the information. In the calculation the copies overlap by 0.9, that is, they are imperfect copies, which on their own distinguish little.
  • At zero copies no local reading exists.

Where the link comes from. From a status report of August, the status report 2.9-2.18 (document QUANTUM_PLACEMENT, §0). Here it is reread in the pre-geometric sense: the sense of a relation that comes before the geometry of space.

A correction. That August text used "non-local" in Bell's sense, that is, for the correlations of entanglement. 2.48 corrected it: in MM the word has the sense of §1, not located in space and time.

What rests on calculations.

  • The count of copies of the status report 2.9-2.18. The same number, 21 copies for a 1 % deficit, appears in 2.62.1. A 1 % deficit means that 1 % of the information is missing.
  • Reading D1 of 2.66, compatible with standard decoherence. 2.66 is the deposit on the mass threshold (§3.7); D1 is its reading for the state of an object.

What remains open.

  • The step "matrix → place" has not been made. That is, it is not derived which correlations follow from the count.

3.6 Probability (way C)

What is observed. Take a plate with two slits, that is, two thin openings. Behind the plate there is a screen.

  • Send a single particle towards the slits. It arrives at a single point of the screen.
  • Now send many of them, one after another. Point after point, the fringes of a wave are drawn on the screen.

Each single arrival is unpredictable. But the probability of each point follows a rule. It is the square of a number called the amplitude, more precisely of its size (we see this further below). This is the Born rule.

An example with numbers chosen by us: if the amplitude of an outcome is 0.5, its probability is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25, that is, one case in four.

Known physics. In known physics the Born rule is a postulate: it is assumed, not derived.

Some have tried to derive it by counting branches or copies (Zurek 2005). A branch is one of the possible outcomes, seen as a history of its own. These derivations rest on a contested step: equal copies, equal probabilities (Schlosshauer and Fine 2005).

What the non-local phase does. For the author the non-local phase and the mirror reciprocity between the two ends of the nexus give a reason for the probabilistic behaviour of matter.

The framework's image is this. One has to describe a 10 having only the 1. Then the 1 must be distributed over ten positions, one for each unit of the 10.

Where the link comes from. From the author's premise of 22 October. It is in the box "MM in brief", the short summary that appears on all MM records.

3.9 Space and time themselves (way A)

What rests on calculations (MM 2.40 and 2.40.2). Some words are needed.

  • The arithmetic mean of two numbers is their sum divided by two. The geometric mean is the square root of their product. An example with numbers chosen by us: for 4 and 9 the arithmetic mean is 6.5; the geometric mean is √36 = 6.
  • The two light sides. Between two ticks of a clock one can draw a diamond made of light rays: one to the right and one to the left. The two sides of the diamond, measured along the directions of light, are the two light sides.
  • The factor √(1 − v²) says how much a moving clock slows down: it is the factor of time dilation. Many books call its inverse, γ = 1/√(1 − v²), the Lorentz factor: the two express the same dilation, one multiplies and the other divides. Here v is the velocity, measured as a fraction of the speed of light. An example with numbers chosen by us: at v = 0.8 the factor is √(1 − 0.64) = 0.6. While 10 seconds pass for someone at rest, the moving clock marks 6.
  • Hermann Bondi was a physicist who presented relativity with signals of light sent and received.

Here are the results.

  • Einstein's time. A clock that reads the number of copies runs at the geometric mean of the two light sides. That is, it runs with Einstein's proper time. Grant the premise of counts on the two sides (as in Bondi). Then the factor √(1 − v²) is an identity: it always holds, given how things are defined.
  • Without assigned places. The copies are placed at random. One takes the longest chain: the longest row of events, each one after the previous one. The chain recovers the factor within about 1 %, in 1+1 dimensions (one of space and one of time).
  • On a grid of places. The same reading gives the arithmetic mean: Galileo's time, that is, the time of physics before Einstein.
  • With fixed places and random times. The dilation is 7 to 29 % stronger than Lorentz's, because the places define a frame.
  • In 3+1 dimensions, that is, three of space and one of time. With about 6,000 copies, clocks with the same proper time are compared. The difference is within 1.3 σ. The symbol σ (sigma) measures statistical uncertainty: a difference of about one σ is of the kind that chance often produces.
  • At high speed. At v = 0.8, with about 780 copies, the deviation exceeds the fixed 6 % threshold. That threshold had been registered beforehand, for the group of tests called block P1-P5.
  • More than two sides. One can also count on more than two sides. Then only the pairwise rule keeps the Lorentz factor. And n sides give n − 1 directions.

A close idea is that of Knuth and Bahreyni (2014).

What remains open.

3.11 Dark matter as a second mirror (way B)

  • Cold means that its particles move slowly.

In way B, then, "it does not exist for us" holds for light and for electromagnetic interactions. It does not hold for gravity.

Glossary

The words of the framework (MM)

  • AI's translation: the way in which a sentence of the author becomes a quantity that can be calculated. It is a choice. An outcome that depends on a translation concerns that translation.
  • Box "MM in brief": the short summary that appears on all MM records.
  • Checkerboard: a model of 2.40 in which a particle advances in steps along the directions of light.
  • Copy: the same unit counted once more. Between two copies there is no distance.
  • Entry page: the deposit that presents the framework (2.27).
  • Explanatory premise (AI): a proposal of the AI to put the author's ideas in order.
  • Fractionation: the dividing of the count into fractions. For the author it acts on the electromagnetic interaction, not on gravity (§3.11).
  • GCD: the common element of the two terms of a nexus. In arithmetic it is the greatest common divisor: for 3 and 5 it is 1.
  • Local phase, matrix: the same reality counted from a point. The multiples of the GCD appear as time, the fractions as space.
  • Loop: the closure of a history between preparation and outcome, in which the future contributes to choosing the past.
  • Nexus: the relation between two distinct terms that have a single element in common, as 3 and 5.
  • Non-local (in MM): not located in space and time. It does not mean at a distance or faster than light.
  • Non-local phase: reality as pure relations, without space, without time and without objects. It is present everywhere, neither before nor beneath the rest.
  • Pre-geometric programme: the project of making the geometry of space arise from the framework, instead of starting from it.
  • Premise: a starting sentence of the author, a fixed point like a postulate.
  • Reading: what an observer obtains from its own matrix. The observer is any part of the structure that records and compares.
  • Record: what an observer has collected.
  • Ways A, B, C: the three sides from which the non-local phase is seen (§2). A: copies without distance. B: existence passes through a link. C: the reading is local and partial.

The words of physics

  • a₀: the common scale of acceleration that galaxies follow at low accelerations.
  • A posteriori: said of a check made after seeing the data.
  • Acceleration: how much a velocity changes in each second.
  • Amplitude: the number that quantum mechanics gives to each outcome. It is a complex number, with a size (the modulus) and a phase. The square of its modulus is the probability.
  • Baryons: the particles of ordinary matter, such as protons and neutrons.
  • Bell limit: the limit that the three assumptions of Bell's theorem, together, put on correlations. For the quantity S it is 2.
  • Bit: the unit of information, that of a yes-or-no answer.
  • Boost: how many times the attraction read exceeds the one predicted by Newton's law.
  • Born rule: the probability of an outcome is the square of the modulus of its amplitude, |ψ|².
  • Correlation: the agreement between two results, more frequent than chance would make it.
  • Decoherence: the loss of interference fringes when the environment carries away copies of where an object is.
  • Entanglement: a state of two or more particles, even distant ones, that must be described together.
  • Fermions: the family of particles to which electrons belong. When two fermions are exchanged the state changes sign.
  • Field: a quantity present at every point of space. In field theory each species of particle has its own field.
  • Gács–Körner common part: the information that two records have in common exactly; each can obtain it alone.
  • Globular cluster: a compact ball of very many stars.
  • Interference fringes: the light and dark stripes that appear where two parts of a wave reinforce or cancel each other.
  • Interferometer: an apparatus that splits a wave over two paths and then joins them again.
  • Light cone: the set of the points of spacetime linked to an event by signals at the speed of light or slower.
  • Loophole: a way in which an ordinary explanation could still account for the results of a Bell experiment.
  • MOND: modified Newtonian dynamics, a theory that changes Newton's law at very small accelerations.
  • Noether's theorem: the theorem that ties the symmetries of the laws to the quantities that are conserved.
  • Parsec (pc): a unit of distance for stars, a little more than three light years.
  • Photon: a particle of light.
  • Proper time: the time marked by a clock along its path.
  • Quantum Darwinism: the idea that a piece of information becomes objective when many copies of it spread into the environment (Zurek 2009).
  • Sigma (σ): the measure of statistical uncertainty.
  • Singlet: an entangled state of two particles with spin. Along the same direction the two measurements always give opposite results.
  • Spin: a property of particles that is measured along a chosen direction. It is not the rotation of a little ball.
  • Strong equivalence principle: in Einstein's gravity, a uniform external field does not change the internal dynamics of a system.
  • Superdeterminism: the idea that the choices of measurement are tied in advance to the state being measured.
  • Superposition: a state that combines two or more conditions, for example two positions. It is not an object in two places at once.
  • Tsirelson bound: the highest value, 2√2, that the correlations of quantum mechanics can reach in the quantity S (Tsirelson's theorem, 1980).
  • Wave function (ψ): the quantum description of a system, which gives the amplitudes.
  • Wigner's friend: a thought experiment. An observer measures inside a closed laboratory; a second observer, outside, treats the whole laboratory as a quantum system.
  • z: how much the light of a distant object has been stretched by the expansion of the universe.

Names of instruments and data

  • Cassini: the space probe that studied Saturn; its data check gravity in the Solar System.
  • DESI DR2: the second data release of DESI, a large survey of galaxies that measures the history of the expansion.
  • Gaia DR3: the third data release of Gaia, the satellite that measures positions and motions of stars.
  • Planck: the satellite that measured the oldest light in the universe.
