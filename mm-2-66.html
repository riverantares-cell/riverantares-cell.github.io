Abstract

Standard quantum mechanics has no mass at which a superposition stops: large objects look classical because of decoherence, the environment that keeps recording where they are. Collapse models (Ghirardi–Rimini–Weber, CSL, Diósi–Penrose) add such a limit by hand. The framework says that a threshold exists: below a certain mass level the principal GCD of the point of observation and the GCD of the diffraction that generates the object become equal, and the object spreads over several positions (see the box above). The box gives no number. This deposit tries to give the threshold a number, or to say clearly why it cannot. A second part tests two later remarks of the author: the limit cannot be fixed as a mass, because a calculation of the «fractional distance» is missing; and large distant bodies stay fixed because their size makes up for the distance. It was announced in deposit 2.64 ( DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23610071 ).

In brief

The question

Small things behave in a strange way. Take an atom, or a molecule. Send it through a set of slits, that is, of thin openings. It does not go through one slit or the other. It goes through all of them at once. On the screen it leaves the stripes of a wave, like the waves of water that pass through two gaps in a dam. Physicists call this superposition: the object is spread over several positions at the same time.

Large things, instead, are never seen like this. A chair is always in one place. Where does the change happen? And why?

What today's physics says

Standard quantum mechanics has no mass at which the change happens. It accounts for the classical look of large objects with decoherence. The environment constantly "looks" at a large object: air molecules, light, heat. So the stripes are washed out before anyone can see them. Think of a drawing made with sand on a table that everyone keeps bumping into. The sand stays, but the drawing is erased at once.

Some physicists think there is more. They think there is a true limit, present even in a perfect vacuum, beyond which a superposition cannot survive. These are the collapse models. The best known ties the limit to gravity (Diósi and Penrose).

What the author's framework says

The author's framework, MM, says that such a limit exists. At the top of every record there is a summary, "MM in brief". There it says that, when an object falls below a certain mass level, two quantities of the framework become equal. They are the "principal GCD" of the point of observation and the "GCD of the diffraction" that generates the object. Then the object spreads over several positions, and we observe it as probability. GCD stands for greatest common divisor. We will explain it in section 2.

That summary gives no number for the threshold. This work tries to give it one. Or it tries to say clearly why it cannot.

After the first part, the author added two remarks. First: the limit cannot be fixed as a mass, because the calculation of a «distanza frazionale» ("fractional distance") is missing. Second: at an adequate distance even a large object could take on quantum features. Large distant bodies, instead, stay fixed, because their size makes up for the distance. The second part tests these remarks.

The five main results

  • For distance, the limit is not a mass alone. There are two readings of the fractional distance that hold. In both, the limit is a ratio. It is the size to be told apart against the "cell" of the copies that do the telling, together with the number of copies. (The readings of the first part with a mass threshold at the Planck scale remain possible. But they are not tested.) The two readings have a similar form. That they are a single rule of the framework is a proposal.
  • For the state of an object the copies are the photons and molecules that the environment carries away. The cell is the scale at which one copy tells two positions apart. For light it is about half a wavelength. Below that scale each copy takes away only a small part of the coherence. This is why a crystal of 16 micrograms stays quantum over 2·10⁻¹⁸ m. To fix it, about 10³⁴ thermal copies would be needed.
  • Take the way in which a distant observer reads an object. Here the cell is the diffraction cell of the observer's aperture, at the distance of the object. Two stars of almost the same mass fall on opposite sides. The disc of Sirius B (1.02 solar masses, 2.64 parsec) is 20-60 times smaller than the finest cell of today's interferometers. This is a calculation from its radius. τ Ceti (0.78 solar masses, farther, 3.65 pc) and 61 Cyg A (0.67-0.74, 3.49 pc), instead, are resolved. This means that their disc is measured. With this instrumental classification the sample does not support a mass limit of the reading, of mass alone, that grows with distance.
  • The reading "what matters for the state of an object is how far the observer is" does not follow from the data. Fullerene molecules are heated, with the same apparatus and the same observer. The interference goes from 47 % to 7 %, then to zero. The cause is photons that nobody collects.
  • Three readings do not follow from the data.
  • The threshold as an internal acceleration compared with the galactic scale a₀ (a reading from the author's book).
  • The threshold at the mass of the proton or of the electron.
  • a₀ written as a mass.

In 2026 clusters of about 7,500 sodium atoms (172,000 atomic mass units) showed interference. They are held together by accelerations 26 orders of magnitude above a₀. That means a hundred million billion billion times larger. A caesium atom has 132 times the mass of the proton. It kept a superposition of 1.9 micrometres for 70 seconds.

  • Five readings remain possible, but they cannot be tested today:
  • two thresholds near the Planck mass: 22 micrograms, and 15.4 micrograms in the "two walls" form;
  • a threshold at the mass of the observer: 70 kg;
  • two thresholds of the Diósi and Penrose type.

Take an object heavier than about 3·10⁻²² kg. No experiment has yet put its centre of mass into a superposition as large as the object itself. In today's interference experiments the effect of gravity is at most 4.2·10⁻¹⁰ of what would be needed to see it.

  • The reading with the clock of deposit 2.40 falls exactly on the 2026 record (ratio 1.24). But it is not a general threshold. It coincides with the design condition of grating interferometers, the Talbot condition. Atom interferometers, where the superposition is created by laser pulses, go beyond it by seven orders of magnitude, that is, by ten million times. It describes a condition of the apparatus, that is, of the reading, not a property of the object.
  • The dated prediction is registered: time 22 October 2026, 13:00:30 UTC; deadline 31 December 2035. A program applies its rules and passes 45 tests out of 45. The heaviest planned experiment is the space mission MAQRO. In the most optimistic study scenario it would reach 0.095-0.35 of the effect of gravity. Within the limits of the published study, at most 0.05. The most likely outcome in 2035 is "not assessable".

No experiment has met a threshold, so far. All the data are explained by standard quantum mechanics with decoherence. The reading "no intrinsic threshold, the threshold is decoherence" is compatible with them. The two rules of the fractional distance that hold coincide with known physics. Known physics here is decoherence, and the wave optics of the light that carries the image. The framework admits them. An experiment can put them to the test. But it cannot tell them apart from standard physics.

1. What is already known

Quantum mechanics has no mass threshold

Nothing in the theory stops a superposition when the object grows. Large objects look classical because of decoherence (Joos and Zeh 1985; Zurek 2003; Schlosshauer 2019). Every collision with a gas molecule or with a photon carries away a trace of where the object is. And the stripes disappear.

For large objects this happens in very short times. Take a speck of dust spread over a hundredth of a millimetre. Decoherence takes about 10⁻³¹ s in air. In the best laboratory vacuum it takes about 10⁻¹⁴ s (Schlosshauer 2019, Table 1). For a decoherence threshold the environment decides, not the mass.

Collapse models put in a threshold "by hand"

Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber (1986) proposed a model, called GRW. There is also a continuous version, called CSL (Pearle 1989; Ghirardi, Pearle and Rimini 1990). These models add to quantum mechanics a small random process. The process localises objects, that is, it fixes them in one place. For one atom it is rare. For many atoms together it is fast. Two numbers fix it: a rate λ and a length r_C.

  • The values proposed by Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber (λ = 10⁻¹⁶ s⁻¹ at r_C = 10⁻⁷ m) are not excluded yet.
  • Larger values are excluded. They are excluded by the emission of X-rays in germanium detectors and by mechanical experiments (Carlesso and others 2022; the review by Carlesso and Donadi 2025).

Diósi (1987, 1989) and Penrose (1996) tie the collapse to gravity. A superposition of two positions of a mass lasts about ħ/E_G. Here E_G is the gravitational energy of the difference between the two arrangements of the mass. The symbol ħ is the reduced Planck constant, the basic number of quantum mechanics.

  • Take the version in which there is no number to be adjusted by hand. This version of the model is excluded by an underground experiment at Gran Sasso (Donadi and others 2021).
  • There is a version with a radius R₀ over which the mass of each nucleus is spread. The same kind of measurement requires R₀ > 2.54·10⁻¹⁰ m (Majorana Demonstrator 2022, as corrected by its erratum).

Older still is the work of Károlyházy (1966).

How "quantum" an experiment is

Nimmrichter and Hornberger (2013) proposed a number: the macroscopicity μ. The larger μ is, the more the experiment restricts any modification of quantum mechanics of the collapse-model type.

Distance, in the experiments (second part)

The separation against the scale of the copies.

  • In an atom interferometer a single scattered photon removes all the contrast when the two paths are about half a wavelength apart. It is "the resolving power of an ideal Heisenberg microscope". Beyond that, the contrast comes back at intervals (Pfau and others 1994, Chapman and others 1995, as reported by Kokorowski and others 2001).
  • With many photons the contrast falls as exp(−κ²d²/2). At fixed d it falls almost exponentially with the mean number of photons (Kokorowski and others 2001, n̄ from 0.9 to 8.2).
  • Fullerenes (carbon molecules shaped like a ball) are heated before an interferometer. The stripes are lost: 47 %, 29 %, 7 %, 0 % at 0, 3, 6 and 10.5 W. The cause is thermal photons that go off into space. The loss follows the wavelength of the photons compared with the separation (Hackermüller and others 2004).
  • With a residual gas the visibility falls as exp(−p/p₀). It agrees with the theory of decoherence, with no numbers adjusted by hand (Hornberger and others 2003).
  • The copies carried away count even if nobody collects them.

The distance between the parts, and from the source of the settings. There are correlations beyond the Bell bound. The Bell bound is the maximum that two objects can have in common if each one follows only local rules. Examples:

  • between atoms at 398 m (S = 2.221 ± 0.033);
  • between electron spins in two diamonds at 1.3 km (2.42 ± 0.20);
  • between photons sent from a satellite to stations 1,203 km apart (2.37 ± 0.09). Here the settings are chosen by the light of stars hundreds of light years away and of quasars billions of years old;
  • entanglement, that is, a quantum link, between two mechanical oscillators 20 cm apart;
  • a "quantum eraser" at 55 m (visibility 0.95) and at 144 km (0.75-0.78, with a link attenuated by 33 dB).

They are different set-ups, not a controlled series. They show quantum behaviour at every separation tried. But none of them changes only the distance between a system and whoever records it.

Wigner's friend. It is a thought experiment, today actually carried out. One observer, the "friend", measures inside a closed laboratory. An outside observer treats the whole laboratory as a quantum system. In the experiments the records of the friends are coded in paths of photons, or in a few qubits of a quantum computer. The outside observers see correlations that would not be allowed if the outcomes of the friends were absolute (with locality and without superdeterminism). The experiments are: Proietti and others 2019, S = 2.416 ± 0.075; Bong and others 2020; Zeng and others 2025; a preprint of 2026. No experiment has a friend with a macroscopic mass.

The reading of distant objects.

  • The light of a distant star is coherent over an aperture smaller than about λ/θ (van Cittert-Zernike theorem). Here θ is the angular diameter. This is why interferometers measure the diameters of stars from the fringes. Examples: α Cen A 8.502 ± 0.038 mas, τ Ceti 2.022 ± 0.010 mas, 61 Cyg A 1.775 ± 0.013 mas. The mas is a thousandth of an arcsecond: a very small angle.
  • The naked eye, adapted to the dark, sees a flash of 54-148 photons at the cornea (Hecht, Shlaer and Pirenne 1942). It sees a single photon just above chance (0.516 ± 0.010, Tinsley and others 2016).

These data concern the reading, not the state of the objects.

2. The thesis, explained

2.1 The framework, in a few lines

In the author's framework the physical world is made of relations. Space and time have only one logical element in common. The author calls it their greatest common divisor (GCD). It is like the numbers 3 and 5, which have only 1 in common. The local world arises when a relation is counted through this GCD. Seen by an observer inside, the multiples appear as time and the fractions as space. The observed objects are linked to the observer by a "diffraction", in fractions, of the counting of whoever observes.

2.2 What the thesis says about quantum mechanics

The summary "MM in brief" published on every record says this. When an observed object is large enough, its mass fixes it in the counting of the point of observation. Then we see it in one place and at one instant. When it falls below a certain mass level, two GCDs become equal, as in a mirror. One is the principal GCD, that of the point of observation. The other is the GCD of the diffraction that generates the object. Then the object no longer occupies a single point. It spreads over several possible positions. And we observe it as probability. The summary adds that the idea of a passage from quantum to classical that depends on mass is close to that of Penrose and Diósi.

Three things in this text are readings by the AI, approved by the author for publication:

  • "principal GCD" = the GCD of the point of observation;
  • "GCD of the diffraction" = that of the diffraction that generates the object;
  • the threshold = the point where the two become equal.

The exact words of the author are in a separate file of the package.

Two later remarks of the author (25 October 2026), told in the words of physics.

  • The first. The mass limit cannot be determined, because a calculation of the «distanza frazionale» ("fractional distance") is missing. At an adequate distance even a macroscopic object could take on quantum features.
  • The second answers an objection: the Moon is seen sharp from 384,000 km. Distant bodies such as the Moon are large enough to stay fixed, even with a high fractional distance. A direct test would be to look at a single stone on Mars with the naked eye, without any instrument.

The author calls the first remark a suspicion to be evaluated.

2.3 What has to be decided to give the threshold a number

  • What "quantum behaviour" means, as something measured. We fixed two observables. Q1: stripes of interference of the centre of mass that differ from the classical prediction. Q2: a state of motion that no classical description allows between distinguishable positions. It is shown by a negative Wigner function.
  • Mass alone is not enough. Collapse models depend also on the separation Δx between the two positions. And they depend on the time T for which the superposition lasts. A threshold must say m*(Δx, T): a mass that depends on separation and time.
  • It must be intrinsic. A threshold of the framework should be there even without an environment. Where decoherence already destroys the stripes, a threshold cannot be seen. And this must be said.
  • What the two GCDs are, in physical quantities. The framework does not say it. Each translation of section 3 makes a choice. The choices are the AI's. An outcome hits that choice, not the author's premise.
  • What "distance" is, and for whom. Two things must be kept apart. The state of the object: is the object itself in superposition? And the reading: how does it appear to an observer who looks at it from far away? The data on the Moon, on the stars and on the eye concern the reading, never the state. Each reading of §3.3 says which of the two it speaks about.

3. How the thesis was tested

3.1 The inventory (Level 0)

Twenty papers were downloaded from arXiv. For each experiment we noted mass, separation, time, environment, outcome and the page where they are written. Before any calculation the experiments were divided into three groups:

  • decisive: E1, E3, E8;
  • supporting, which cannot exclude a reading on their own: E2 and E9, read second hand; E5;
  • not decisive, which show no superpositions beyond the zero-point motion: E4, E6, E7.

For E3 the effective mass is a choice of the authors of the experiment. It is the mass assigned to a vibration mode. And it depends on how its amplitude is defined. For this reason E3 decides a reading only on one condition. The outcome must stay the same when the effective mass changes by a factor of 100, up or down.

3.2 The ten readings (Level 1)

For every pair (reading, experiment) a program computes a ratio r.

  • r < 1 means: the experiment is on the quantum side of the threshold.
  • r > 1 means: it is on the side where the reading says "fixed in one place".

The outcome rules were fixed before the calculation:

  • excluded (it is the registered label; in this text we say "does not follow from the data"): a decisive experiment shows quantum behaviour with r > 10;
  • compatible, put to the test: no exclusion, and a decisive experiment has 0.1 ≤ r ≤ 10;
  • not testable today: no exclusion, and all decisive experiments have r < 0.1.

The factor 10 covers the conventions of order one:

  • the 2π between h and ħ;
  • a factor 2 between the conventions of Diósi and of Penrose;
  • Δx taken equal to the period of the grating.

3.3 The second part: readings of the fractional distance

4.3 The reading with the clock of deposit 2.40 (T2)

The reading. In deposit 2.40 the time of a clock is the geometric mean of its two light sides. The geometric mean of two numbers is the square root of their product. For 4 and 9 it is 6. Here one side is the diffraction side of the object, h/(mc). The other is the light side of the observation, cT. Then the space that the observed point occupies in the time T is √(hT/m). The threshold is where this space equals the separation Δx: m* = hT/Δx².

What the data say. In E1 we take Δx equal to the grating period. And we take T equal to the flight time between two gratings. We find r = 1.24. The record experiment sits exactly on this threshold. With ħ in place of h the ratio would be 7.8. And it would exceed 10 as soon as the true separation were 13 % larger than the period. So the data prefer h. With h the outcome stays the same for separations up to about 2.5 times the period.

Why it is not a general threshold. The condition m = hT/Δx² is exactly the Talbot condition. It is the condition with which a three-grating interferometer is designed. The first grating localises the object at the scale of the period. The stripes form only if the wave spreads at least that much. The idea of T2 was written after reading this in the paper of E1. It is declared as contamination.

In quantum mechanics the coherence of a free object grows as ħT/(mσ₀). Here σ₀ is how localised the object was at the start. T2 is the case σ₀ ≈ Δx, up to numerical factors. Where the superposition is created by laser pulses, quantum mechanics goes far beyond T2. Take the atom interferometer of Kovachy and others, with 54 cm and at most 3 s. There r ≥ 2.1·10⁷ (an experiment read second hand). In E8, in the phase in which the separation of 1.9 μm is built (0.267 ms), r = 4.5.

Outcome. With the registered rules T2 remains "compatible, put to the test". But the reading "m* = hT/Δx² is the threshold of quantum behaviour" does not follow. A narrower reading remains: T2 describes the condition of grating interferometers. It is a threshold on the reading, not on the object. T2 is not identical to quantum mechanics. It is a particular case of it, which quantum mechanics exceeds.

4.4 Decoherence (T4)

Take the collisions with the residual gas in E1. If the gas is air at the stated pressure, they remove about a quarter of the coherence (r = 0.29). They remove about two thirds if the gas is hydrogen (r = 1.08). Hydrogen is the usual residual gas of an ultra-high vacuum. It is the reading "threshold on the reading, not on systems", proposed in August. It coincides with standard quantum mechanics by construction. The second part gives it a rule, D1 (§4.6). 5.1 Which papers count

A paper counts if it meets all these conditions:

  • its first version on arXiv (or in the journal, if that comes first) has a date after the time limit. The date does not go beyond 31 December 2035;
  • it reports an experiment, not a proposal;
  • it gives the mass, the separation, the time, and when needed the size and the material;
  • it gives the observable (Q1 or Q2) with its significance.

Confidence levels and p-values are converted into standard deviations with fixed formulas. The target is the first paper, in order of date, with a ratio of at least 0.1.

5.2 The rules

  • Planck block (T1, T1b), r = m/m*. The prediction: above m* no quantum behaviour of the centre of mass. If quantum behaviour is seen at 3σ or more:
  • "compatible, put to the test" for 0.1 ≤ r < 1;
  • "tension" for 1 ≤ r ≤ 10;
  • "excluded" for r > 10, whatever the visibility.

"Favourable" only if all these things hold:

  • quantum behaviour is not seen at 3σ or more;
  • r ≥ 1;
  • the stripes vanish above m* (fraction of the expected visibility f + 2σ_f < 0.2);
  • in the same apparatus, with a mass of at most m*/2, the stripes are there (f − 2σ_f > 0.5), with no known cause given by the authors.

For a vibration mode the outcome must hold for every effective mass within a factor of 100.

  • Gravity block (T3a, T3b), r = E_G·T/ħ. The prediction: the superposition is reduced by a factor exp(−r), beyond the known decoherence. If quantum behaviour is seen at 3σ or more:
  • "compatible" for 0.1 ≤ r ≤ 1;
  • "tension" for 1 < r ≤ 10;
  • "excluded" for r > 10. "Excluded" also if the observed fraction of visibility is clearly above what the reading allows (f − 2σ_f > 10·exp(−r/2)).

"Favourable" if:

  • r ≥ 1;
  • the observed fraction of visibility is clearly below one (f + 2σ_f < 0.8);
  • the fraction lies in the band allowed by the reading with a factor 2 on r (exp(−2r)/3 ≤ f ≤ 3·exp(−r/2));
  • the authors give no known cause for the loss.

In this block the rules on f come before those on σ. The reason is that T3 predicts a reduced interference, not an absent one.

Glossary

  • a₀: the acceleration scale of galaxies, about 1.2·10⁻¹⁰ m/s². Below this acceleration the rotation of galaxies departs from Newton's law.
  • Atomic mass unit (u): 1.66·10⁻²⁷ kg, about the mass of a proton; 1 kDa = 1,000 u, 1 MDa = 10⁶ u.
  • Cat state: a superposition of two clearly different states of a large system.
  • Centre of mass: the point that moves as if all the mass were there. A superposition of the centre of mass means that the whole object is in two places.
  • Collapse model: a modification of quantum mechanics that makes superpositions end by themselves. It makes them end faster for larger objects (GRW, CSL, Diósi-Penrose).
  • Compton wavelength: h/(mc), the length linked to a mass m. The reduced one is ħ/(mc).
  • Contamination: what was known of the result when a rule was written.
  • Copy: in the author's framework, each photon or molecule that carries away a trace of where an object is.
  • CSL: continuous spontaneous localisation, the continuous version of the GRW model. It has a rate λ and a length r_C.
  • Decisive, supporting, not decisive: the three groups into which the experiments were put before the calculation.
  • Decoherence: the loss of the stripes caused by the environment, which records where the object is.
  • Diffraction cell: the smallest angle that an aperture of size a can tell apart, about 1.22 λ/a. Multiplied by the distance, it gives the smallest size that can be told apart there.
  • Diósi-Penrose model: a superposition of two positions of a mass lasts about ħ/E_G. There is a version with a radius R₀ for the nuclei.
  • Effective mass: the mass assigned to a vibration mode. It depends on how the authors define its amplitude.
  • E_G: the gravitational energy of the difference between the two arrangements of the mass in a superposition.
  • Fractional distance: a term of the author. In this work, the size to be told apart measured in the cell of the copies of whoever reads (§3.3).
  • GCD: greatest common divisor. In the author's framework it is the only logical element that space and time have in common. "Principal GCD" and "GCD of the diffraction" are terms of the framework (§2.2).
  • GRW: the collapse model of Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber (1986).
  • Interferometer: an apparatus that splits a wave and recombines it, so that the parts can interfere. A Talbot-Lau interferometer uses three gratings.
  • Local friendliness: inequalities that hold if the outcomes observed by "friends" in closed laboratories are absolute, the agents are free and the influences are local. Quantum mechanics violates them (Bong and others 2020).
  • Macroscopicity μ: a number that says how much an experiment restricts the modifications of quantum mechanics of the collapse-model type (Nimmrichter and Hornberger 2013).
  • MAQRO: a proposed space mission for interference with particles from 10⁷ to 10¹¹ u.
  • mas: milliarcsecond, 4.85·10⁻⁹ rad.
  • Planck mass (m_P): √(ħc/G) = 2.18·10⁻⁸ kg, about 22 micrograms.
  • Ratio r: for each reading and experiment, how far the experiment is from the threshold of the reading; r < 1 on the quantum side.
  • Reading (L) and state (Q): how an object appears in the record of an observer, and what the object itself does. The second part keeps them apart.
  • Registered: written in a file whose SHA-256 fingerprint and UTC time were noted before the calculation. The file is not changed afterwards.
  • Resolved: an object whose angular size an instrument can measure. Unresolved objects appear as the diffraction figure of the instrument.
  • Schwarzschild radius: 2Gm/c², the radius at which a mass m would become a black hole.
  • Standard deviation (σ): the measure of statistical uncertainty; 3σ corresponds to a confidence of 99.73 %.
  • Superposition: the state of an object spread over several positions (or states) at the same time.
  • Talbot condition: the condition under which a three-grating interferometer forms the stripes. Between two gratings the wave must spread at least as much as the period of the grating.
  • van Cittert-Zernike theorem: the light of a distant, incoherent source of angular diameter θ is coherent over a transverse distance of about λ/θ. For a uniform disc the visibility of the fringes with baseline B is |2J₁(x)/x|, x = πθB/λ.
  • Visibility: the contrast of the stripes, from 0 (none) to 1.
  • Wigner function: a description of a quantum state in position and momentum. Where it is negative, no classical description is possible.
  • Wigner's friend: a thought experiment, today carried out with photons and qubits. An observer (the friend) measures inside a closed laboratory. An outside observer treats the laboratory as a quantum system.
