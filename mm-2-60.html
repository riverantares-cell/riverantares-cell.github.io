The Cassini measurement

The Solar System lies inside a galaxy, the Milky Way. The gravity of the Galaxy pulls the Sun and the planets. This gravity, which comes from outside, is called the external field. A field says how much and in which direction gravity pulls, at every point of space.

There is an idea in physics called MOND. We introduce it shortly. In MOND the external field changes the Sun's gravity a little. It stretches it along the direction of the Galactic centre. And it squeezes it at the sides. A deformation of this kind is called a quadrupole.

An everyday example: the tides. The Moon stretches the Earth's oceans along the line that goes from the Earth to the Moon. So the water rises on two opposite sides of the Earth. This deformation is a quadrupole too.

Cassini was a space probe. For many years it went round Saturn. From Earth it was followed with radio signals. This is called radio tracking. In this way Saturn's orbit is known very well. A quadrupole around the Sun would change that orbit a little.

The Cassini measurement puts a limit on the quadrupole. Its value is called Q₂ (Q-two):

Q₂ = (1.6 ± 1.8)·10⁻²⁷ s⁻²

  • The sign ± (plus or minus) gives the error of the measurement. The true value probably lies between 1.6 minus 1.8 and 1.6 plus 1.8. Zero is inside this band.
  • 10⁻²⁷ is a tiny number. It means one divided by a one followed by twenty-seven zeros.
  • s⁻² means divided by a second squared.
  • The measurement is by Park and colleagues (Park et al. 2026, in the journal PRD; arXiv:2602.17884). Et al. is Latin and means and others. arXiv is the site where physicists put their papers online.

The QUMOND chain taken to the Sun

First a little history. In galaxies the stars far from the centre go round faster than expected. Visible matter alone, with Newton's law, is not enough to keep them in orbit so fast. There are two kinds of answer:

  • add an invisible matter, dark matter;
  • change the law of gravity when the acceleration is very small.

Acceleration says how fast the speed changes. MOND is the second answer. It is an idea of the physicist Milgrom. In MOND there is a special, tiny acceleration: a₀ (a-zero). When Newton's acceleration is much larger than a₀, Newton holds. When it is smaller, gravity is stronger than Newton's.

QUMOND is a version of MOND written with a field equation. A field equation computes gravity at every point of space, even in empty space.

MM 2.56 had built a chain. It is a row of four choices, linked like the rings of a chain.

  1. a₀ = cH₀/6. Here c is the speed of light. H₀ (H-zero) is the Hubble constant: it says how fast the universe is expanding today. In words: a₀ is the speed of light times the Hubble constant, divided by six.
  2. The law ν(y) = 1/(1 − e^(−√y)). The letter ν (nu) gives the boost. The boost is the number by which Newton's gravity is multiplied. The letter y is Newton's acceleration divided by a₀. The letter e is Euler's number, a constant of mathematics. In words: when y is large, ν is almost 1, and Newton holds. When y is small, ν grows.
  3. The Milky-Way field, V²/R. V is the speed at which the Sun goes round the centre of the Galaxy. R is the Sun's distance from the centre. In words: the speed squared divided by the distance. It is the acceleration that keeps the Sun on its orbit around the Galaxy.
  4. QUMOND, with its field equation.

Taken down to the Sun, the chain predicts:

Q₂ = 3.22·10⁻²⁶ s⁻²

  • This value lies 17σ above the measurement. The letter σ (sigma) stands for the typical error of the measurement. 17σ means a distance of seventeen typical errors. It is a very large distance.
  • The starting choices can be made in eight different ways. In all of them the distance goes from 17.0σ to 19.4σ.

An example with numbers chosen by us. Someone predicts that an empty scale will show 17 grams. You look at it: it shows zero. The scale is usually off by 1 gram, up or down. The prediction then lies 17 typical errors away from the measurement: 17σ. That prediction does not hold.

The outcome. Cassini excludes the global-field reading down to the Sun. It is the reading of the chain: the field of the Galaxy enters all of space through the field equation, down to the Sun. This reading falls. The outcome hits this reading, not the MM framework.

The local reading (section 4)

The record proposes another reading: the local reading. First we need three words.

  • Read, reading. In MM a body is said to read a field when its motion takes it into account. A reading is a rule of this kind. It is an interpretation, written so that it can be put to the test.
  • Anomaly. It is the part of gravity in excess of Newton.
  • MCD. It is the greatest common divisor. At school it is used with numbers. An example: the MCD of 6 and 9 is 3. In the MM framework the MCD is the common unit that a relation counts. In this record the local MCD of a mass is a precise number: R_s/r.
  • R_s is the Schwarzschild radius of the mass. It is the radius that a black hole with that mass would have.
  • r is the distance from the mass.
  • In words: the local MCD is large near a large mass. And it decreases as one moves away.

In the local reading the anomaly does not live in empty space. It lives in the relation between two bodies. A relation is the link between two bodies that attract each other, like the Sun and a planet. The reading has six rules:

  1. Who reads the external field. The body whose local MCD prevails, that is, is larger, reads the external field for its system.
  2. The dominant compact body. A body is dominant when it prevails. It is compact when it is small compared with the distance of the relation. A dominant compact body reads the relation itself.
  3. Without a dominant body. If no body prevails, the relation is read along its orbit.
  4. Interior and exterior. The interior field and the exterior field add as magnitudes. That is, their values are added, without looking at the directions.
  5. The direction. It enters through a tensorial alignment: what counts is the angle between the pair of stars and the centre of the Galaxy. How much it counts is said by the data: it is a measured amount.
  6. Light. Light reads the curvature and not the external field.

We look at the rules one by one in section 4.

What the local reading gives

With these rules the local reading gives these results.

  • Saturn: Q₂ ≈ 10⁻³⁰ s⁻². The sign ≈ means about equal. 10⁻³⁰ is much smaller than 10⁻²⁷, the order of the Cassini measurement.
  • Planets and comets, with any orbit: they are Newtonian. It means that they follow Newton's law, with no boost.
  • The star S2. It goes round Sgr A*, the black hole at the centre of the Milky Way. For S2 there is no anomalous boost. General relativity remains, Einstein's theory of gravity.
  • Wide binaries. They are pairs of stars that go round each other, very far apart. MM 2.56 had chosen 919 of them at low acceleration. For them the local reading gives a boost ⟨η⟩ = 1.27. The letter η (eta) stands for the boost; the brackets ⟨ ⟩ stand for the mean. The QUMOND chain gives 1.32. Newton gives 1, that is, no boost.
  • Disc galaxies. They are flat galaxies that rotate, like the Milky Way. For them the radial acceleration relation stays unchanged. It is an observed law. It links the measured acceleration of stars and gas to the acceleration that visible matter alone would give. Radial means along the radius, towards the centre.
  • The environment field. Each galaxy also feels the gravity of nearby galaxies. It is its environment field. In 78 galaxies of the SPARC catalogue this field enters in full, as in the binaries. SPARC is a catalogue of galaxies whose rotation is well known.
  • Weak lensing. Gravity bends light, like a lens. So a nearby galaxy slightly deforms the images of the distant galaxies behind it. When the deformation is small one speaks of weak lensing. KiDS-1000 is a large survey of the sky that measures it. Here χ² is 219.9 on 60 points, against 1,687.8 for Newton. χ² (chi-squared) measures how far a model is from the data: the smaller it is, the better.
  • Galaxies in denser environments. They are galaxies with many other galaxies nearby. Around them light reads two to three times more at large radii, that is, far from the centre. The comparison is between all the galaxies that act as lenses and the isolated ones.
  • Red and blue. A central galaxy does not go round another, larger one. Among isolated central galaxies, red and blue ones differ by a nearly constant ratio. So the excess is compatible with the satellites of the full sample, rather than with a reading of light that grows with density. Satellites are small galaxies that go round a larger galaxy.

Systems without rotation (section 4.9)

In some systems the stars do not go round in order, as in a disc. They move in all directions, like the bees of a swarm. The record looks at two kinds of such systems:

  • dwarf spheroidal galaxies: small ball-shaped galaxies;
  • globular clusters: balls of old stars, much smaller than a galaxy.

There are three results.

  • The classical dwarf spheroidals, that is, the best known, agree within ±0.15 dex. Agree means that prediction and measurement match.
  • The ultra-faint dwarfs are very small and dim. They move 2.5 to 20 times faster than the reading predicts.
  • Distant globular clusters are Newtonian.

The dex measures ratios in powers of ten. 1 dex means ten times. 0 dex means equal. An example with numbers chosen by us: 0.30 dex means about double.

At the same gravitational state. Here the gravitational state is the number x_h + e_N. x_h is the interior gravity of the system, and e_N is the external field. Both are divided by a₀. We look at them more closely in section 4.9.

  • The systems with x_h + e_N between 0.019 and 0.12 are taken. They are 19 dwarfs and 11 clusters.
  • The galaxies carry the boost: they sit +1.0 dex above Newton.
  • The clusters do not: they sit at +0.02.

The mirror. The text speaks of the dark-matter mirror: in the MM framework it is the name of what is usually attributed to dark matter. A system carries a mirror when the mass obtained from the motion of its stars is much larger than the visible mass (section 4.9).

  • The systems whose stars belong to different generations, that is, were born at different times, carry a mirror. This is seen from the metallicity spread.
  • Metallicity is the amount of elements heavier than helium in a star. Spread means that the value changes from star to star.
  • Single-generation clusters do not carry a mirror.

How much mirror. The amount follows a common profile, set by the radius: M_dyn ∝ r_½^1.54±0.11.

  • M_dyn is the dynamical mass: the mass obtained from the motion of the stars.
  • r_½ is the radius that contains half of the light of the system.
  • The sign ∝ means proportional to. In words: the dynamical mass grows as the radius raised to the power 1.54, with an error of 0.11.
  • The profile holds for satellites and for isolated dwarfs alike.
  • Around the profile there is an intrinsic scatter of 0.48 dex. Intrinsic means that it does not come from the measurement errors.

1. What was already known and what is new

This section separates the work done by others from the new work of this record.

What was already known.

  • The external-field quadrupole in MOND. It was studied by Milgrom (2009) and Blanchet and Novak (2011), in modified-Poisson MOND. The Poisson equation is the field equation of Newton's gravity. In this form of MOND the equation is changed.
  • The Cassini constraints. A constraint is a limit set by the measurements. They were written about by Hees et al. (2014 and 2016) and Park et al. (2026).
  • Cassini and wide binaries. AQUAL is another version of MOND with a field equation. Within QUMOND and AQUAL, Cassini implies a Newtonian wide-binary test. In words: in these theories, if Cassini does not see the quadrupole, wide binaries must turn out Newtonian too. This was shown by Desmond, Hees and Famaey (2024), Desmond (2025) and Park et al. (2026).
  • Comets. Long-period comets take a very long time to go once round the Sun. These comets disfavour AQUAL: Vokrouhlický, Nesvorný and Tremaine (2024). Disfavour means that the data go against it, without excluding it completely.
  • Orbit-dependent readings. They are also called modified-inertia readings. Inertia is a body's resistance to changing its speed. These readings are Milgrom's.
  • The external field in galaxies. The external-field effect in SPARC rotation curves: Chae et al. (2020, 2021). A rotation curve says how fast stars and gas go round at each distance from the centre of the galaxy.
  • Weak lensing. The radial acceleration relation measured with weak lensing: Brouwer et al. (2021).

What is new here.

  • The chain's number. It is the value of Q₂ predicted by the chain. It is obtained with no input taken from the binaries. The solver of MM 2.56, that is, the program that solves the equations, reproduces it within 3·10⁻⁵.
  • The pairs, one by one. The 919 pairs are located one by one in the region where the quadrupole is generated.
  • One local reading holds together four things:
  • Saturn, comets and S2;
  • wide binaries;
  • disc galaxies in their environment;
  • weak lensing.
  • A check of dwarf galaxies and distant globular clusters, with the same reading.
  • The two groups split: the galaxies are boosted, the clusters are Newtonian.
  • Who carries the mirror and how much are measured. The systems with stars of different generations carry it. The amount follows a common profile set by the radius.
  • Its profile for DR4 is registered.
  • A dipole statistic for wide binaries. The systematic effects of the sample do not reach it. A systematic effect is an error that always goes the same way, like a badly calibrated scale. We look at the dipole in section 4.6.

2. The data used

Here are the measured data used in the record, one by one, with their source.

The quadrupole Q₂.

  • Its effect on gravity is written δΦ = −(Q₂/2)[(ê·x)² − r²/3].
  • δΦ (delta phi) is the small change of the gravitational potential. The potential says how much gravitational energy each kilogram of matter has at a point.
  • ê is the direction towards the Galactic centre. x is the position and r the distance from the Sun.
  • In words: the effect depends on how much the position is aligned with the direction of the centre.
  • The value is (1.6 ± 1.8)·10⁻²⁷ s⁻². Source: Park et al. 2026, equation 6, table 1.

The Solar-System acceleration.

  • It is (2.32 ± 0.16)·10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻². It is the acceleration with which the whole Solar System falls towards the Galactic centre.
  • m s⁻² means metres per second squared. It is the unit of accelerations.
  • Source: Gaia EDR3, Klioner et al. 2021. EDR3 is a first part of Gaia's third data release.

The Milky-Way field used by the chain.

  • V²/R = 2.1456·10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻², with V = 233 km/s and R = 8.2 kpc.
  • The parsec (pc) is a unit of distance used for stars. A kiloparsec (kpc) is a thousand parsecs.
  • Source: MM 2.56.

The scale a₀ = cH₀/6.

  • With H₀ = 67.4 it is 1.0914·10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻². With H₀ = 73.04 it is 1.1827·10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻².
  • Two values of H₀ are used, which come from different measurements. H₀ is measured in kilometres per second per megaparsec.
  • Sources: MM 2.54 and MM 2.56.

The baryonic surface density.

  • Σ_b = 47 M☉ pc⁻². It is how much ordinary matter there is in the disc of the Galaxy, around the Sun, per square parsec.
  • Baryonic means made of ordinary matter, the matter of atoms. M☉ is the mass of the Sun, used as a unit.
  • Source: Park et al. 2026, after Bland-Hawthorn and Gerhard 2016.

The pairs of stars.

  • They are 919 low-acceleration pairs (s > 5000 au, x < 1) and 21,471 control pairs.
  • s is the separation, that is, the distance between the two stars. The au (astronomical unit) is the mean distance between the Earth and the Sun.
  • x is the Newtonian acceleration of the pair divided by a₀. x < 1 means that this acceleration is below a₀.
  • The control pairs are closer, with higher acceleration. They serve for comparison.
  • Source: MM 2.56 (El-Badry, Rix and Heintz 2021).

The eccentricity law of the pairs.

  • p(e) ∝ e^α, with α(s) of Hwang et al. 2022.
  • The eccentricity e says how flattened an orbit is. 0 is a circle. Near 1 it is a very elongated ellipse.
  • p(e) says how frequent each eccentricity is. α(s) is an exponent that changes with the separation s.
  • As in MM 2.56.

The supplementary perihelion precessions.

  • The perihelion is the point of a planet's orbit closest to the Sun. Precession is a slow rotation of the orbit. Supplementary means in addition to the expected one.
  • Source: EPM2011 (Pitjeva and Pitjev 2013), with the INPOP10a uncertainties (Fienga and Minazzoli 2024).
  • EPM2011 and INPOP10a are ephemerides: large tables that give the positions of the planets.

Light deflection.

  • The Sun's gravity bends the light that passes close to it. The number γ (gamma) says how much it bends it. In general relativity γ = 1.
  • Measurement: γ − 1 = (2.1 ± 2.3)·10⁻⁵.
  • Source: Bertotti, Iess and Tortora 2003 (via Will 2014).

The planetary elements.

  • They are the numbers that describe the orbits of the planets. Here: J2000, table 1. J2000 is the reference date.
  • Source: the approximate positions of JPL, a NASA laboratory.

Weak lensing.

  • The KiDS-1000 lensing rotation curves. They are in 4 mass bins, with 60 points and the full covariance.
  • The covariance says how much the errors of different points go together.
  • Source: Brouwer et al. 2021 (as in MM 2.44).

The rotation curves. SPARC, tables 1 and 2. Source: Lelli, McGaugh and Schombert 2016.

The environment field of the SPARC galaxies. log e_N,env, table 3. Source: Chae et al. 2021 (arXiv:2109.04745).

The wide binaries for the direction.

  • They are 57,583 pairs beyond 5,000 au, and the catalogue beyond 300 au.
  • Source: El-Badry, Rix and Heintz 2021 (VizieR J/MNRAS/506/2269). VizieR is an archive of astronomical catalogues.
  • The catalogue was queried in August 2026.

The dwarf galaxies of the Local Group.

  • The Local Group is the group of galaxies to which the Milky Way belongs.
  • The data are: σ★, V, E(B−V), r_h, ellipticity, distance modulus.
  • σ★ is the velocity dispersion of the stars: how different their velocities are from one another.
  • V is the magnitude in the colour band V, that is, how bright the system appears.
  • E(B−V) measures how much the dust in space, between us and the system, reddens the light.
  • r_h is the radius that contains half of the light.
  • The ellipticity says how flattened the system is.
  • The distance modulus is a way of writing the distance.
  • Source: McConnachie 2012, version of January 2021 (CADC, a Canadian archive of astronomical data).

The globular clusters.

  • The data are: σ_0, mass and M/L_V (for L_V), r_h, R_GC.
  • σ_0 is the velocity dispersion at the centre.
  • M/L_V is the ratio between the mass and the light. L_V is the light in a colour band called V.
  • R_GC is the distance from the Galactic centre.
  • Source: the Baumgardt database, combined table of January 2026.

Dwarfs and clusters with metallicity spreads.

  • The data are: σ_v, σ[Fe/H], r_½, M_V, distances, host.
  • σ_v is the velocity dispersion.
  • σ[Fe/H] is the metallicity spread, measured with iron.
  • M_V is the absolute magnitude, a measure of luminosity.
  • The host is the galaxy around which a system goes.
  • Source: Local Volume Database (Pace 2025), version of 12 August 2026.

4. The local reading

This is the central part of the record. Here the rules of the local reading are presented one by one, with their checks.

4.1 Where the anomaly lives

  • In QUMOND the anomaly is a phantom density in the empty space around a mass. Around the Sun it forms a cloud thousands of au wide. This cloud generates the quadrupole excluded by Cassini.
  • In the local reading, instead, the anomaly lives in the relation between two material bodies.
  • The phantom cloud is a tool of the QUMOND equation, not an object.

An everyday example. On maps there are meridians and parallels. They serve to do calculations and to find one's way. But on the ground those lines are not there.

4.2 Who reads the external field: the body whose local MCD prevails

The rule. The local MCD of a mass is R_s/r.

  • In a relation there are two bodies. The MCD of each is taken at the place of the other, that is, at the same distance r.
  • The Schwarzschild radius is proportional to the mass. The ratio of the two MCDs is therefore the mass ratio.
  • The body whose MCD prevails reads the external field for its whole system.

An example with numbers chosen by us. One body has a mass 10 times larger than another. At the same distance, its MCD is 10 times larger. It is the one that prevails.

The Solar System. The Sun's MCD at a planet exceeds the planet's MCD at the Sun. It exceeds it by as many times as the Sun's mass exceeds the planet's:

  • 1,048 times for Jupiter;
  • 3,499 times for Saturn;
  • more for the other planets;
  • about 10¹⁷ times for a comet.

Planets and comets read the Galaxy through the Sun. So the Sun and Saturn receive the same acceleration from the Galaxy.

  • Without this rule, the two accelerations would differ by 6.7·10⁻¹¹ m s⁻².
  • As a scale estimate: δa/n² ≈ 1,500 km for Saturn's orbit. Here δa is that difference in acceleration. The letter n is Saturn's angular speed, that is, how fast it goes round its orbit. In words: the scale of the shift would be about 1,500 km.
  • Radio tracking, instead, measures at the level of metres.

Galaxies. A galaxy prevails over its stars and its gas. The mass ratios go from 10⁶ to 10¹¹. 10⁶ is a million. Stars and gas read the environment field through the galaxy.

Wide binaries. In the pairs the mass ratio is at most 7.4. So each star reads the Galaxy itself.

The threshold. What does prevailing mean? Any threshold between 7.4 and 1,048 will do. No number has to be tuned. In words: 7.4 is the largest ratio between the stars of the binaries. 1,048 is the smallest between the Sun and the planets. Any threshold in between separates the two cases.

4.3 A dominant and compact body reads the relation itself

The rule. Sometimes a body prevails and is also compact with respect to the relation. That is, its radius, R_dom, is much smaller than the distance r. Then the relation is read with the MCD of the dominant body at its own place: its surface.

  • There the reading is very high: for the Sun x = 2.5·10¹². Remember: x is Newton's acceleration divided by a₀.
  • So the relation is Newtonian. With x so large, the boost ν is practically 1.

An extended dominant body is, for example, a galaxy around its stars and its gas. It is spread along the relation. So the reading stays local.

The compactness threshold. R_dom/r is the ratio between the radius of the dominant body and the distance. Any threshold between two values will do.

  • 7·10⁻⁴: it is the case of Sgr A* and S2 at pericentre, the point of the orbit closest to the black hole. It is the least compact of the compact cases.
  • 2.5·10⁻²: it is the case of the Milky Way with a satellite at 100 kpc. It is the most compact of the extended cases.

What it gives.

  • Planets and comets on any orbit are Newtonian. This also holds for the new comets of the Oort spike. They are comets that have never passed close to the Sun. The Oort cloud is a large cloud of comets at the edges of the Solar System.
  • S2: no anomalous boost. So general relativity remains, as measured.
  • Wide binaries: no star prevails. So the boost remains.
  • Galaxies: the radial acceleration relation remains. This also holds for the outer gas and for the satellites.

4.4 A relation without a dominant body: the inertial state

The rule. If no body prevails, the reading of the relation is its time average along the orbit:

x_eff = ⟨x⟩ = x(a)/√(1 − e²), with x = GM/(r² a₀).

  • x is Newton's acceleration, GM/r², divided by a₀. G is the gravitational constant, M the mass, r the distance.
  • a is the semi-major axis: half of the longest diameter of the ellipse of the orbit.
  • e is the eccentricity of the orbit.
  • In words: the effective reading, x_eff, is the x computed at the semi-major axis. Then it is divided by the square root of (1 minus e squared).

An everyday example. The temperature changes during the day. But to say what a day was like, the mean temperature is often used. Here the same is done with x, along the whole orbit.

  • The rule applies to relations in which no body prevails, such as wide binaries.
  • On DR3 the orbit average fits the data better than the instantaneous reading, that is, the one taken point by point (section 5).
  • On circular orbits x_eff = x. So the radial acceleration relation of disc galaxies is unchanged.

The DR3 gain sits in the fast pairs. On DR3 the gain over Newton comes entirely from the fast pairs.

  • The pairs are divided into u classes. The number u measures how fast a pair is.
  • The third with the highest u gives +10.6 of a gain of about +11. It is an average over four series of simulations.

A reading that falls. Another reading can be thought of.

  • In the fast phases of the orbit the centrifugal force prevails over the gravity read. The centrifugal force is the outward push that one feels on a bend, for example in a car.
  • This reading would make the fast phases Newtonian. It would remove the boost exactly there.
  • The data exclude it. The registered criterion gives −10.4 (s 3.2), instead of a positive value. Here −10.4 is the average over the four series of simulations, and s is the standard deviation: how much the result changes from one series to another. This reading falls.
  • The u classes alone do not say in which phase of the orbit a pair is.

In words: the interior reading of the pair, x_eff, is added to the external field, e_N. Then the law ν is applied.

  • the sum of magnitudes: the values are added, without looking at the directions;

An example with numbers chosen by us. Two pushes are worth 3 and 4, and they are at a right angle. Added as arrows they give 5. Added as magnitudes they give 7.

4.6 Direction: a tensorial alignment

Here the direction of the pair with respect to the Galactic centre comes into play.

The Galactic MCD difference between the two stars. The star nearer the Galactic centre reads a higher Galactic MCD.

  • Along the line joining the two stars, the difference is 2 g_e s cos θ/c².
  • Here g_e is the external field and s the separation. θ (theta) is the angle between the pair and the direction of the centre. c is the speed of light.
  • The pair's own MCD is 2GM/(s c²).
  • Their ratio, q = (g_e/a₀)/x, sets the weight of the direction. Careful: this q is not the mass ratio of section 4.5. It is another number, with the same letter.
  • The median of q is 3.2 for the 919 wide pairs. It is 0.018 for the close pairs. The median is the value that sits in the middle: half of the pairs have a smaller q, half a larger one.

The reading is a tensorial alignment. A tensor is a mathematical object that takes two directions into account together.

  • Here there are two directions: ŝ, the line of the pair, and ê, the direction of the centre.
  • The line of the pair contracts with the direction of the centre: ŝᵢŝⱼêᵢêⱼ = cos²θ. To contract means to combine the two directions into a single number.
  • In words: the result is the square of the cosine of the angle between the two directions. It is 1 when the pair points towards the centre. It is 0 when the pair lies crosswise.

More MCD read means more speed:

v² = v₀² (1 + w q cos²θ).

In words: the square of the speed, v², is the starting one, v₀², multiplied by (1 plus w times q times cos²θ). The boost is largest when the pair is aligned with the Galactic centre.

The amount w is an observational input. That is, it is measured from the data, not derived from the theory.

  • The data of August 2026 give w ≈ 0.007. The interval at 2σ goes from 0.001 to 0.014. This value comes from a 3D statistic, that is, one made in three dimensions.
  • From the quadrupole on the sky comes instead w = 0.024 ± 0.022.
  • With w ≈ 0.007 the mean boost of the wide pairs changes by +0.002 dex. So section 4.5 is not affected by it.
  • The simulations registered for DR4 do not include this term.

The computation redone with the program for DR4.

  • The program for DR4 was run on DR3. It subtracted the level of the close pairs. And it used the full covariance (section 5.5).
  • It gives w = 0.005 ± 0.010 in the symmetric form, and −0.003 ± 0.009 in the dominant form.
  • These values are compatible with zero. The upper limits are 0.024 and 0.014.
  • The August values, written above, contained the sample level of the close pairs.

Two words for the sky: dipole and quadrupole. On the sky one looks at an angle, φ (phi), between the pair and the direction of the Galactic centre.

  • A cos φ term makes a difference between the side towards the centre and the opposite side. It is called a dipole.
  • A cos 2φ term is the same on the two opposite sides, and different crosswise. It is called a quadrupole. It is like the tide, which raises the water on two opposite sides of the Earth.

The form is symmetric. Imagine the companion behind the star, on the far side from the centre. The star in front still moves with the anomaly, because of the companion. The boost is there on both sides. So on the sky there is only the cos 2φ term, and the dipole is zero.

The alternative form is registered too. It is read by the dominant star, as in section 4.3.

  • Here the boost is there only when the secondary star lies between the primary and the centre. The primary is the main star of the pair, the secondary its companion.
  • This would give a dipole (cos φ) about 1.9 times the quadrupole.

The data of August 2026. They are 9,378 pairs between 5,000 and 50,000 au, in the region w > 0.5.

  • The dipole is +0.0027 ± 0.0046 dex. The quadrupole is +0.0050 ± 0.0046 dex.
  • In the close pairs (300–2,000 au) the quadrupole is +0.0041 ± 0.0018 dex, where the prediction is zero. The dipole is +0.0001 ± 0.0018 dex.
  • The Galactic north pole is used as a control direction. It is the direction perpendicular to the disc of the Milky Way. Nothing is seen there.

The dipole is clean. Gaia observes the sky following a fixed path, its scanning law. Neither this law nor the selection of the pairs knows which star is the brighter with respect to the Galactic centre.

The rule. Light reads the curvature of the MCD, with the same law as the stars, without extra mass. The curvature says how much a field changes from one point to another, not only how much it is worth.

Light does not read the external field. A uniform field, that is, the same at every point, has no curvature. Matter instead reads relations, and there the external field enters.

An everyday example. In a lift in free fall, you and the objects fall together. From inside, a uniform field is not noticed. Only the differences from one point to another are noticed.

5.1 The test of the boost

The law in the simulations. The simulations compute the orbits of the pairs on a computer.

  • Each relative orbit is Keplerian, that is, an ellipse like those of the planets, with G_eff = ν(x_eff + e_N).
  • G_eff is the effective gravitational constant, measured in units of Newton's constant G. So G_eff = ν means: the gravity of the pair is Newton's multiplied by the boost ν.
  • The same programs as in MM 2.56 are used. Only the force law and the file paths change.

The library. It contains 200,000 orbits, computed with seed 18 in 337 s (seconds).

  • In a Keplerian orbit the energy stays constant. In the computation its relative change, |dE/E|, is 4.5·10⁻¹³ at the 99th percentile. It means that in 99 per cent of the orbits the change is smaller than that.
  • Each orbit is Keplerian.
  • No pair starts unbound, that is, with the stars free to move apart for ever.

The check of the copies. The programs are copies of those of MM 2.56. With the six hypotheses of MM 2.56, the evaluation reproduces the published dry run exactly: D = +7.64, subsets +7.56 / +4.50 / +2.07. A dry run is a rehearsal on data already seen, to check that everything works.

The decision rule. D_L = L(local reading) − L(Newton).

  • L is the log-likelihood. In words: D_L says by how much the local reading does better than Newton.
  • The simulations are done in several replicas, that is, several times. D_L,k is the same quantity, redone with one replica removed. The subsets are the values obtained in this way.
  • Local reading favoured: D_L ≥ +5 and every D_L,k ≥ +2. The rule asks that the result stays at least +2 even when any one replica is removed.
  • Newton favoured: D_L ≤ −5 and every D_L,k ≤ −2. The sign ≤ means less than or equal to.
  • Otherwise: inconclusive, that is, the test does not decide.
  • Not valid: if a validity condition of MM 2.56 is not met.

Informative numbers only, which do not decide:

  • F_L = L(local reading) − L(chain): by how much the local reading does better than the chain;
  • the slope statistic S, over the two halves of the low band.

5.5 The test of direction: the anisotropy

The model. v² = v₀² (1 + w q cos²θ), as in section 4.6.

  • The number w is measured in each of three wide bands: 5,000–10,000, 10,000–20,000 and 20,000–50,000 au.
  • The projection on the sky uses the orbits of the MM 2.56 forward model.

Consistency. The three values must agree: χ² with 2 degrees of freedom, p > 0.05. The degrees of freedom count the independent comparisons. Moreover the common w must be positive.

The sample effects. In the 300–2,000 au band the reading predicts zero. The level measured there is subtracted, as a constant.

An everyday example: the tare. Before weighing the flour, you weigh the empty bowl. Then you take away its weight.

The dipole is the primary statistic to tell the two forms apart.

  • In the form read by the dominant star: positive, about 1.9 times the quadrupole.
  • In the symmetric form: zero.
  • The close-pair dipole must be zero. The Galactic north pole is the control direction.

Addendum 2. The reading predicts the symmetric form: a zero dipole in every band, with all the anisotropy in the quadrupole.

  • A positive dipole of about 1.9 times the quadrupole would favour the alternative.
  • On DR3 the dipole is +0.0027 ± 0.0046 dex.

The code was registered before the DR4 release.

  • It reads the files written by the MM 2.56 data conversion.
  • The dry run on DR3 gives S_ORB = 0.166 dex, as in August.
  • The close pairs show B2 = +0.0037 ± 0.0015 and B1 = 0.0000 ± 0.0015. S_ORB, B2 and B1 are quantities computed by the program. Here B1 and B2 are not the series of section 5.3.
  • The outcome of the dry run: w compatible with zero in both forms (limits 0.024 and 0.014).

6. Predictions the data will read

These are the predictions of the record. The data will say whether they hold.

Gaia DR4.

  • The boost of wide binaries, and its rise with separation.
  • The anisotropy. It must be positive, and only in wide pairs. It must grow as q ∝ s²/M. In words: it grows with the square of the separation, and it decreases if the pair is heavier.
  • The same w in every band, and no change of sign. Full QUMOND, instead, changes sign near 12,000 au.
  • A zero dipole (symmetric form). A positive dipole would favour the reading by the dominant star.
  • No significant dependence of the boost on the mass ratio, below the prevalence threshold. This check will be made on many more unequal pairs.

Systems without rotation.

  • Distant globular clusters Newtonian.
  • New dwarfs with stars of different generations, on the same profile: M_dyn ∝ r_½^1.5.
  • New single-generation clusters Newtonian.

Light companions.

  • Think of a companion much lighter than its star, at a wide separation. For example a brown dwarf, that is, a failed star, too small to light up. Or a planet at 10⁴ au, that is, at ten thousand au.
  • This companion is read by the star itself, and it is Newtonian.
  • Pairs with comparable masses are boosted.
  • Between the mass ratios 1/1,048 and 1/7.4 the threshold will be set by the data.

Comets and the Oort cloud: Newtonian, on any orbit.

Weak lensing: no external-field effect around galaxies, in any environment. The dynamics of stars and binaries, that is, their motion, does show it instead.

7. What this record does not contain

This section says what the record does not claim and does not do.

  • A covariant field theory of the local reading. A covariant theory has the same form for all observers, like general relativity. Here the local reading is a law of motion for bound relations, that is, for bodies that go round each other. To this a reading of light through the curvature is added.
  • The direction term inside the DR4 simulations of the boost. Its mean effect is +0.002 dex.
  • A derivation of the common profile of the dwarfs. Its exponent and its normalisation are measured, not obtained from the theory. The normalisation is the level of the profile: it says how large the mass is at a given radius.
  • A change to the MM 2.56 protocol. The record changes nothing in that protocol.

Glossary

  • a₀ (a-zero). A tiny acceleration. Below a₀, in MOND, gravity is stronger than Newton's. In the chain it is cH₀/6.
  • Acceleration. How fast the speed changes. It is measured in metres per second squared (m s⁻²).
  • Anisotropy. An effect that changes with direction.
  • Anomaly. The part of gravity in excess of Newton.
  • au (astronomical unit). The mean distance between the Earth and the Sun.
  • Boost (ν, η). The number by which Newton's gravity is multiplied. ν is the law that gives it; η is its value for a pair.
  • χ² (chi-squared) and Δχ². χ² measures how far a model is from the data: the smaller it is, the better. Δχ² is the difference between two χ².
  • Control pairs and low band. The control pairs are close, with high acceleration. The low band contains the 919 low-acceleration pairs.
  • Covariance. How much the errors of two measurements go together.
  • Curvature. How much a field changes from one point to another. A uniform field has no curvature.
  • Deviance. A number that measures how far a hypothesis is from the data, like χ².
  • dex. A way of measuring ratios in powers of ten. 1 dex is a factor of ten; 0 dex means equal.
  • Dipole and quadrupole on the sky. The dipole (cos φ) makes a difference between one side and the opposite side. The quadrupole (cos 2φ) is the same on the two opposite sides.
  • Dominant, compact, extended body. Dominant is the body whose local MCD prevails. It is compact if it is small compared with the distance of the relation, like the Sun. It is extended if it is spread along the relation, like a galaxy.
  • Dry run. A rehearsal on data already seen, to check that everything works.
  • Dwarf spheroidal and ultra-faint galaxies. Small ball-shaped galaxies. The ultra-faint ones are the smallest and the least bright.
  • Dynamical mass (M_dyn). The mass obtained from the motion of the stars.
  • Eccentricity. How flattened an orbit is: 0 is a circle, near 1 it is a very elongated ellipse.
  • Environment field. The gravity of nearby galaxies, felt by a galaxy.
  • External field. The gravity that comes from outside a system. For the Solar System it is that of the Milky Way.
  • Field. It says how much and in which direction gravity pulls, at every point of space.
  • Forward model. A computation that starts from the law of gravity and predicts what should be seen in the data.
  • Gaia and its data releases. Gaia is a satellite that measures the position and the motion of stars. DR3 is its data release already published. The next release is called DR4.
  • General relativity. Einstein's theory of gravity.
  • Global-field reading. The reading of the chain: the field of the Galaxy enters all of space through the field equation, down to the Sun. Cassini excludes it.
  • Globular cluster. A ball of old stars, much smaller than a galaxy. Its stars were born nearly together.
  • Gravitational state (x_h + e_N). The sum of the interior gravity of the system and the external field, divided by a₀.
  • Inertial state. The rule for relations without a dominant body: the reading is the time average along the orbit.
  • Intrinsic scatter. The spread of the data around a law that does not come from the measurement errors.
  • Likelihood and log-likelihood. The likelihood says how probable a hypothesis makes the observed data. The log-likelihood is its logarithm. The higher it is, the better.
  • Local reading. The reading proposed in this record: the anomaly lives in the relation between two bodies, with six rules.
  • MCD. The greatest common divisor. In the MM framework it is the common unit that a relation counts. In this record the local MCD of a mass is R_s/r.
  • Median. The value that sits in the middle: half of the cases are below, half above.
  • Metallicity. The amount of elements heavier than helium in a star. If it changes from star to star, the stars are of different generations.
  • Mirror. In the MM framework, the name of what is usually attributed to dark matter (the text says dark-matter mirror). A system carries a mirror when its dynamical mass is more than three times the visible mass: B = M_dyn/M_b > 3.
  • MOND, QUMOND, AQUAL. MOND is Milgrom's idea: below a₀ gravity is stronger than Newton's. QUMOND and AQUAL are two versions of MOND written with a field equation.
  • MOND radius (r_M). The distance at which the Newtonian acceleration of a body drops to a₀. For the Sun it is 7,371 au.
  • Newtonian. Following Newton's law of gravity, with no boost.
  • Noise and seed. The seed is the starting number of the random numbers of a simulation. The noise is how much the result changes when the seed changes.
  • Oort cloud. A large cloud of comets at the edges of the Solar System.
  • Orbit library. A large set of orbits computed on a computer for the simulations.
  • Outcome. The result of a test. A negative outcome makes a reading fall, not the MM framework.
  • parsec, kpc, Mpc. The parsec (pc) is a unit of distance used for stars. A kpc is a thousand parsecs, a Mpc a million parsecs.
  • Phantom density. In QUMOND, the invisible mass that seems to be in empty space. It is a tool of the equation, not an object.
  • Protocol. The list of the steps of a test, written before doing it.
  • Quadrupole (Q₂). A deformation that stretches along one direction and squeezes at the sides, like the tide. Cassini measures its value around the Sun.
  • QUMOND chain. The row of four choices of MM 2.56: a₀ = cH₀/6, the law ν, the Milky-Way field V²/R, the QUMOND equation.
  • Radial acceleration relation. An observed law in disc galaxies. It links the measured acceleration to the acceleration of visible matter alone.
  • Radio tracking. Following a probe from Earth with radio signals, to know its position.
  • Reading, read. In MM a body reads a field when its motion takes it into account. A reading is a rule of this kind: an interpretation, which can be put to the test.
  • Registered. Written and fixed with its date before seeing the data, so that it cannot be changed afterwards.
  • Relation. The link between two bodies that attract each other, like the Sun and a planet, or the two stars of a pair.
  • Rotation curve. How fast stars and gas go round, at each distance from the centre of a galaxy.
  • Satellite. A small galaxy that goes round a larger galaxy.
  • Schwarzschild radius (R_s). The radius that a black hole with a given mass would have.
  • σ (sigma) and z. σ is the typical error of a measurement. z says by how many σ a prediction lies away from the measurement.
  • Standard deviation. How far the values lie, on average, from their mean.
  • Systems without rotation. Systems in which the stars move in all directions, like the bees of a swarm.
  • Tensorial alignment. The way in which the direction enters the local reading. What counts is the square of the cosine of the angle between the pair and the Galactic centre: cos²θ.
  • Total field and baryonic field. The baryonic field comes from ordinary matter alone. The total field also includes the dark-matter mirror.
  • Velocity dispersion. How different the velocities of the stars of a system are from one another.
  • Weak lensing. A small deformation of the images of distant galaxies, due to the gravity of nearer galaxies.
  • Wide binaries. Pairs of stars that go round each other, very far apart.
  • x and x_eff. x is Newton's acceleration divided by a₀. x_eff is its average along the orbit.
