Abstract

Bell experiments without loopholes give CHSH values above the local bound 2, and high-precision measurements are compatible with Tsirelson's quantum bound 2√2 (Poh et al. 2015: 2.82759 ± 0.00051). This record asks what the author's framework, Mathematical Materiality (MM), must contain to respect both facts, and where it then stands among interpretations of quantum mechanics. No mathematical priority is claimed: the theorems used are published (Tsirelson; Barnum et al. 2010; Sorkin's quantum measures; Craig, Dowker et al. 2007; Aharonov's two-time formalism; Cramer; Argaman 2010; Wharton and Argaman 2020), and the record separates published results, derivations in the working notes and numerical conjectures. Predictions were registered with SHA-256 fingerprints before each test; failed predictions are reported. (1) Local readings and the Tsirelson bound. If each wing is read with the Born geometry (Euclidean state space with all its effects), no no-signalling joint event exceeds 2√2. Replacing the Euclidean ball with ℓ^p balls raises the maximum: 2^(2−1/p) for p ≥ 2 (the value of Ver Steeg and Wehner 2009), above 2√2 also for 1 < p < 2 (exact value conjectural). With marginals of length m the maximum is the quantum 2√(2 − m²). Two registered predictions failed: a hand formula, wrong for p < 2, and a three-dimensional search that stayed below the value already reached by the two-dimensional construction.

1. What this work is, and what it is not

A link in the MM programme

This work is a link in the MM programme: the one that concerns quantum mechanics. It shows how four working assumptions fit together (section 3). For each one it reports numerical consistency checks, registered beforehand. And it discusses their relation to published experimental results. The four assumptions are:

  • local Born readings;
  • weights built from pairwise correlations;
  • a future boundary that selects the outcome, a "loop";
  • the imaginary weight of the turn, on the checkerboard of MM 2.40.

We will make these words clear in section 3.

What it is not

It is not a derivation of quantum mechanics. And it does not distinguish MM from standard quantum mechanics with an experiment.

For the Born rule there is one more result, in section 4.6 (new in 2.48.1). In the 2.40 model the count of copies of equal modulus gives |ψ|², for rational ratios, that is, fractions. Three declared assumptions are needed: H-es, H-eq and H-add. For irrational ratios H-cont is also needed. The calculations cover denominators up to 12. For arbitrary fractions the general construction of the cited sources is used.

No mathematical priority is claimed: the text does not say that it was the first to discover these mathematical results.

Three kinds of statement kept apart

  • results published by others (section 2);
  • derivations written in the working notes; several were supplied by the verifier;
  • numerical conjectures, that is, results found on the computer but not proved.

Some predictions are registered: written in a file and fixed with a fingerprint before the tests. So they cannot be changed afterwards. The results found afterwards are marked as such. The predictions that failed are reported as failed (sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.3). Each block, and this text, was checked by an independent artificial intelligence system.

2. Prior work, first

An idea to understand: the score S

Before the table, an idea that is needed throughout the text. Let us take two particles born together, which then move apart. Two experimenters, usually called Alice and Bob, measure them, each on their own side. Each can choose between two ways of measuring. Each measurement gives +1 or −1. With many trials one calculates a score, S, which says how much the results of the two sides agree. It is the CHSH combination, from the names of Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt.

  • If the two particles carry with them a common "instruction sheet", written at birth, S does not exceed 2. It is the Bell limit, the local bound.
  • Quantum mechanics goes up to 2√2, about 2.83. It is the Tsirelson bound.
  • A theory that forbids only sending messages faster than light could go up to 4. It is the no-signalling maximum (the "PR boxes").

Experiments find values above 2 and up to about 2.83. The basic question is: why exactly 2√2, and not 4?

3. The working assumptions

The assumptions come from the author of the framework as premises. His exact words are kept outside this deposit. Here they are written in technical form, with what the translation adds.

First, two basic words. In quantum mechanics every possible outcome has an amplitude. Think of it as an arrow: it has a length and a direction. The Born rule says that the probability of an outcome is the square of the length of the arrow: |ψ|². An example with a number chosen by us: an arrow of length 0.6 gives probability 0.36. The singlet is a state of two linked particles: if the spin of the two is measured in the same direction, the results are always opposite.

  • W0 – local Born reading. Each measured system is read with the Born rule of a two-level system, like a quantum coin. Its states form a Euclidean ball: the Bloch ball (d = 3) or its real disc (d = 2), with all its dual effects. "Euclidean" means that distances are measured as in everyday geometry, with Pythagoras' theorem. As a control, section 4.1 replaces the Euclidean ball with real ℓ^p balls. In these, distances are measured in another way: state ‖r‖_p ≤ 1, effects ‖u‖_q ≤ 1, 1/p + 1/q = 1. With p = 2 the normal ball is recovered. B(p) denotes the maximum value of S for d = 2.
  • W1 – weights from copies correlated in pairs. The weight of an event, that is, of a set of "copies", depends only on the single copies and on the pairs: μ(A) = Σ_{α,β∈A} D_αβ. It is a grade-2 measure in Sorkin's sense. Added in translation: D is positive semidefinite (a Gram matrix), so μ(A) = ‖Σ_{α∈A} v_α‖². The premise suggests these two steps, but does not impose them.
  • W2 – the loop. The outcome written in a record is selected by the future end of its history. The history closes on itself, like a ring. In section 4.3 this is a future boundary chosen at random with a certain measure. In section 4.4 it is a hidden parameter that depends on a future setting.
  • W3 – the imaginary weight of the turn. On the checkerboard of 2.40 a particle moves forward in a zigzag. A turn has weight −i sin θ. A straight step has weight cos θ. Here θ is the mass per tick, and i is the imaginary number, the one with i² = −1.
  • A word not to be misunderstood. In MM texts "non-local" means not located in spacetime, pregeometric. It concerns the deepest level of the framework. The events of experiments, the settings and the records are located in spacetime as usual. "Non-local" does not mean an influence faster than light.

4. Tests and results

4.1 Local readings and the Tsirelson bound

The question. Why does S stop at 2√2? This section shows that the cap comes from the way each side is read, on its own.

The model. There are two wings, with the local spaces of W0. The joint event is any matrix C, with marginals, that gives probabilities that are never negative for every pair of local effects. This is called the maximal tensor product, without signalling. The marginals are the averages of the results of one side only, looked at on its own. To look for the maximum of S without constraints, one can take the marginals to be zero and the observables unbiased, without losing anything. Then the constraint becomes ‖C‖_{q→p} ≤ 1. The case with marginals of fixed length m is treated separately below. H = [[1,1],[1,−1]].

The derivations (in the working notes, checked by the verifier).

  • Upper bound. S ≤ ‖v₀+v₁‖_q + ‖v₀−v₁‖_q ≤ 2^(2−1/max(p,q)), by Clarkson's inequalities, in any dimension.
  • p = 2. The bound is 2√2 for every admissible joint event. It comes from the parallelogram law: it is Tsirelson's proof.
  • p ≥ 2. The bound is attained by C = 2^(−1/p)H (Riesz–Thorin with complex endpoints). So B(p) = 2^(2−1/p).
  • 1 < p < 2. B(p) > 2√2 (the proof was supplied by the verifier). The upper bound is not attained. The best value found is the Hadamard construction 2^(1+2/p)/‖H‖_{q→p}. That it is the maximum is a conjecture.
  • Marginals of length m (p = 2, d = 2). The maximum is exactly 2√(2 − m²), the quantum value (proof supplied by the verifier). S = 2√2 forces both marginals to be zero.

The maximum of S for d = 2. It is proved for p ≥ 2 and at the endpoints. For 1 < p < 2 these are the best values found on the computer.

  • T5 failed as written. The random search in d = 3 stayed below the d = 2 value. At p = 3 it fell short by 4.8·10⁻⁴, outside the tolerance 10⁻⁴: this case fails, and with it the test. At p = 1.5 it fell short by 9.4·10⁻⁵ in relative terms (inside the tolerance) or by 2.9·10⁻⁴ in absolute terms (outside). At p = ∞ it matched.
  • The registered tolerance did not say whether it was relative or absolute. The program used the relative one.
  • The search did not try the known construction without changing it.
  • The second round, R2-A, shows that the construction placed in d = 3 reproduces the d = 2 value to 3·10⁻¹⁶. No candidate in d = 3 exceeded it. This, however, does not prove that for p < 2 the result does not depend on the dimension.

What it means. Within the declared model (two sides, maximal tensor product), each side read with the Euclidean geometry (with all its dual effects) gives |S| ≤ 2√2. The cap comes from the local geometry, not from what links the two wings. The measured value, S = 2.82759 ± 0.00051, is compatible. These data, however, do not measure p. An ℓ^p reading would allow S above 2√2, but would not require it.

4.2 Two slits on the checkerboard

The model. The Dirac walk of 2.40 is used, with θ = 0.5. There are two slits at ±20 and a screen 1000 steps later. Three "coins" are tried, that is, three rules for the steps:

  • the coin of 2.40, with −i sin θ on the turns;
  • a real rotation coin, without imaginary numbers;
  • a coin with positive weights only.

Registered outcomes.

  • First run, passed:
  • positive amplitudes never give a negative cross term;
  • the real coin reproduces the probabilities of 2.40 to 3.3·10⁻¹⁵, for sources in one direction and for their mixture (G = diag(1, i) takes one coin into the other);
  • the third-order term |I₃|/max|I₂| = 8·10⁻¹⁶ for the quadratic reading. In this statistic the variants |ψ|^p with p = 1 and 3 give 0.80 and 0.39. It is not the normalisation used by Sinha and colleagues;
  • the matrix of the cross-correlations is positive (by construction).
  • First run, passed with limits:
  • T3b compares the same vector of numbers in the two models, not corresponding preparations: it gives no information;
  • T5b compares the minimum fringe ratio over different regions.
  • First run, not met: the dark fringes (T2) and the fringe spacing (T6). The registered "central region" contained only 2 sites of the lattice. The reason: the weight of a single slit has its peak on the ballistic front, that is, on the two fronts that move away from the slit at constant speed, not in the middle. The definition was wrong, not the code.
  • Second run, registered after seeing the first, with the two slits lit in the same way (all passed):
  • minimum ratio μ_AB/(μ_A + μ_B) = 4.1·10⁻⁴;
  • dark fringes at −143.2 and +140.4 sites (lattice sites −144 and 140), against ±142.1 predicted by the stationary phase with the lattice dispersion cos ω = cos θ cos k;
  • separation 283.6 against 284.3;
  • with positive weights the ratio never goes below 1.000000.

What it means.

  • On the checkerboard every phase comes from the turns. At equal length, two more turns multiply the amplitude by −tan²θ. The minus sign makes two contributions to the same outcome cancel each other: it is destructive interference. This is how the dark fringes arise. Amplitudes that are only positive cannot produce a dark fringe. Think of two water waves: if a crest meets a trough, the water stays flat. With only crests, the water never stays flat.
  • For the preparations tested here, an equivalent real coin reproduces the probabilities. General comparisons require transforming the preparations and the measurements in the same way too.
  • Li and colleagues distinguish real quantum theory from complex quantum theory in a network with independent sources, under the composition assumptions of that test (that is, on how systems are combined). These calculations do not address that question.

A declared defect. The diagnosis script imported the first program and rewrote its results file. The physical values are identical to the registered log. Only the running time changed. The second round, R2-C, does the same diagnosis in a temporary folder and gives the same output.

4.3 Can a future boundary give Born frequencies?

The idea. Here the assumption W2, the loop, is tested. Imagine that the outcome of a measurement is chosen not only by the past, but also by how the history "closes" in the future. Are the frequencies obtained those of the Born rule?

The model.

  • The "forward" state on the screen comes from the checkerboard, with the screen divided into 8 intervals.
  • The "backward" walker uses the adjoint step U† = C†S†. Its coin has +i sin θ on the turns.
  • The future is a minimal space made of record + environment. The rules for choosing the future are compared by their distance from the Born weights w_b. The distance used is the total variation distance (TVD): 0 means equal, 1 completely different. The w_b are conditioned on arrival in [−900, 900] and renormalised over the 8 intervals, like the frequencies.

Registered outcomes (all numerical thresholds met). Forward × backward is equal to the Born weight to 3·10⁻¹⁶. But this is a mathematical identity, not a physical choice.

  • Deviation from the protocol. The first run widened the two outer intervals of the screen. The values in the table come from the run with the registered intervals. 0.74 % of the weight falls outside the screen.

Found after the run, not registered: the exponential race.

  • Take independent exponential random variables with the same rate, E_b. Choosing argmin_b(E_b/w_b) draws branch b with probability w_b/Σ_c w_c. It is like a race: the time of each branch is divided by its weight, and the smallest time wins. So a branch with more weight wins more often.
  • The squared components |Φ_b|² of a Haar-random complex vector (with dimension ≥ number of branches) are variables of this kind, divided by a common sum. So the rule "smallest |Φ_b|²/w_b wins" reproduces Born (TVD 0.0024).
  • It is an exact sampling algorithm. But it already contains the weights.

What it means. In this minimal model a future boundary gives the Born frequencies with some rules (the one weighted with the closure, or the exponential race), and not with others. The measure over futures remains a postulate. In the rule with prior ∝ d_b the factor d_b cancels against the mean overlap 1/d_b.

4.4 A singlet model in which the future acts on the past, along local paths

The idea. In the singlet the two particles give linked results, even if they are far apart. Can one build a model in which nothing goes faster than light, but the future acts on the past? The model of this section adapts to spin ½ a model by Argaman (2010). It is called retrocausal: a cause in the future, an effect in the past.

The model.

  • The source is at the point (0, 0) of the checkerboard. The readings are at (∓L, T). The choices of the settings are made at time T − 10. They are "space-like" separated from the reading of the other wing: no signal at the speed of light can go from one to the other in time. The checkerboard gives only the shape of the paths. The law that depends on the angle is assumed.
  • Alice's future outcome fixes a parameter λ ∈ {a, a + π}, with probability p. The parameter is carried back along Alice's path to the source. Then it goes forward along Bob's path, as λ + π.
  • Bob reads it with the local Born rule cos²((λ_B − b)/2).
  • In exact form, with m = 2p − 1 and c = cos(a − b): P(A = u, B = v) = (1 + um)(1 − uvc)/4. So E = −c for every p, ⟨A⟩ = m and ⟨B⟩ = −mc.
  • At p = ½ these are exactly the singlet probabilities for projective measurements in the same plane.
  • For other values of p, E does not change. But the marginals allow signals to be sent.

Registered outcomes (all passed).

  • E within 0.0042 of −cos(a − b) on a 12×12 grid (threshold 0.008), and S = −2.8281.
  • No observable signal at p = ½: 0.0039 and 0.0040 (threshold 0.008).
  • Signal equal to |2p − 1| for p = 0.6, 0.75, 0.9: 0.202, 0.502, 0.801 (tolerance 0.01).
  • A "forward"-only control, with sign responses, gives |S| = 2.0000.
  • The check of the shape of the paths is passed (with L = T the paths are straight).
  • The symmetric mixture, in which the future of one wing or the other chooses, coincides with −cos(a − b) within 0.0037.

Second round R2-B (all passed).

  • Born responses with λ uniform and independent of the settings: E = −½ cos(a − b) within 0.0055, |S| = 1.4157.
  • Real zigzags with T > L: λ stays constant along every path in 20,000 histories out of 20,000.
  • Screening: with (λ_B, b) fixed, Bob's frequency does not depend on (a, A). The maximum is 1.58 standard errors over 8 comparisons, with threshold 5.
  • Symmetric versus asymmetric version, compared directly: within 0.0046 (threshold 0.012).

What it means.

  • With a dependence on future inputs, ρ(λ | a, b) ≠ ρ(λ), and a local Born reading, the singlet correlations are reproduced. Every link of the chain runs along paths inside the light cones, through the common past of the two readings.
  • In this family of models signals cannot be sent if and only if the future chooses with the balanced weight, the Born one. This link is already discussed by Argaman (2010). And the cos² law of the angle is assumed.
  • The cancellation at p = ½ is a "fine-tuning" in the sense of Wood and Spekkens: a precise value that makes an effect vanish.
  • The parameter must not be readable at the source. Read there, it would reveal a, up to π.
  • What it covers: one singlet pair, projective measurements in the same plane, ideal detectors.

4.5 Self-consistency and the balanced weight

The question. In section 4.4 the balanced weight p = ½ is put in by hand. Why exactly that weight? The author's premise answers that it is not a "why" question: it is the existence of the present that chooses its past. Put technically: only self-consistent histories exist (Novikov; Friedman and colleagues 1990).

Think of the famous grandfather paradox. If you travel into the past and prevent your own birth, who set out? Novikov says: only histories without contradictions can exist.

The calculation (binomial distribution computed numerically, without random draws).

  • In the model of 4.4 with p > ½, Alice encodes a bit x in her setting (a = 0 or π, b = 0). A bit is a piece of yes/no information.
  • N is odd. The N pairs are assumed independent and identical, given x. Then Bob's frequency of B = +1 is ½ ∓ ε/2, with ε = |2p − 1|. Bob decodes x by majority. For p < ½ the decoder must be inverted; the grid uses p ≥ ½.
  • An assumption, not calculated. A faster-than-light channel is assumed which, combined with a second one in another frame of reference, brings the decoded bit back into Alice's past, without other errors. It is Tolman's "antitelephone" (Tolman, The Theory of the Relativity of Motion, 1917; Benford, Book, Newcomb, Phys. Rev. D 2, 263, 1970). The pair model does not provide this construction.
  • The paradox protocol. Alice sets x = NOT(decoded bit): she does the opposite of what she receives. A history is consistent only if Bob decoded NOT x.
  • W(N, p) = P(decoded bit = NOT x) is the weight of the consistent histories, calculated before imposing consistency.

What it shows, and what it does not.

  • Shown. With any weight other than Born's, a protocol with enough pairs makes the prior weight of the consistent histories (the weight before conditioning on consistency) as small as one likes. With the Born weight the channel carries no information. So with the Born weight the antitelephone cannot be built at all, and W = ½ is only a formal comparison.
  • Not shown. That a weight other than Born's is impossible. For 0 < p < 1 and every finite N, W > 0: the consistent histories always have a non-zero weight. (At p = 0 or 1, W = 0 already with one pair.)
  • What is needed to pass from "arbitrarily improbable" to "cannot exist". One more postulate is needed. A formulation close to the author's premise is: a present exists only if no protocol can make its consistency arbitrarily improbable. Here it is checked only for the family of protocols described (inf over N of W > 0). It is assumed that the return link and the resources are available for N as large as one likes. It is written as a postulate of the author, not derived.
  • Another way to impose consistency: conditioning. When W > 0, P_C(h) = P(h)·1_C(h)/W gives the consistent histories total probability one. But it changes the statistics. The post-selected closed timelike curves of Lloyd and colleagues (PRL 106, 040403, 2011) exclude the histories with zero post-selection amplitude and renormalise the others. Which measure describes the closed loop is part of the postulate.
  • What it covers: one protocol, within the pair model. The local cos² law is assumed throughout.
  • Who had already said it. Signals outside the Born weights are known for pilot waves (Valentini, Phys. Lett. A 158, 1, 1991) and for this toy model (Argaman 2010). The reading "existence of the present ⇒ balanced weight" is the author's.
  • Registration. The prediction file was written before the code. Both got their fingerprint in the same minute, before the run.

4.6 Born probabilities by counting copies of equal modulus (new in 2.48.1)

The question. With which declared assumptions does the counting of copies, in the 2.40 model, give the probability p = |ψ|²? And where does the square enter?

The author's premises. Three sentences of the author are kept in Italian, with his exact words, in a file of the package. The archived text gives them translated into English; here they are in that translation:

  • U1 (23 October 2026): if you have to describe a 10 by means of a number 1 (10 = 1), you are forced to distribute the descriptor 1 over 10 space-times of the number 10;
  • U2 (13 October 2026): the rule is decided by the number and the mutual correlation of the copies of the nexus;
  • U3 (13 October 2026): it is the future of a history that chooses its past, so the answer is the loop.

Their translations into mathematical objects are choices of the AI (the "bridges" P1–P6 of the protocol), not words of the author.

Prior work, first. Twenty-two sources were read with the same eight questions: 19 downloaded from arXiv, 3 already in the working records. The derivations that count copies (Zurek 2003, 2005, 2018; the ancilla step of Deutsch 1999; Saunders 2004; Sebens and Carroll 2018) have three ingredients in common:

  1. equal modulus, equal probability. It is the step that the critics attack. For Schlosshauer and Fine (2005) it is an additional assumption about the connection between state and probabilities. For Barnum, Caves, Finkelstein, Fuchs and Schack (2000) Deutsch's version hides a principle of insufficient reason. Envariance gives a symmetry; equal probabilities need a further, contested link;
  2. dividing into copies of equal modulus, and counting. An amplitude √(m/M) becomes m copies of modulus 1/√M, and the probability is m/M. None of the critics contests this step;
  3. the square comes from the conserved norm, not from the count. Zurek attributes it to Pythagoras, that is, to the Euclidean geometry of Hilbert space. Saunders shows that with an evolution that conserves an l^p norm the same argument would give |c|^p.

An example, with the numbers of test K2 (in the table further below). An amplitude √(3/4) is divided into 3 copies, each of modulus 1/√4 = ½. The amplitude that remains, √(1/4), is 1 equal copy. Out of 4 equal copies, 3 belong to the first outcome: probability 3/4. And it is exactly the square of √(3/4).

Other routes:

  • the decision-theoretic derivations (Deutsch; Wallace 2007, 2009) use other axioms: branching indifference, dependence on the state alone, continuity;
  • the structural routes do not count branches: Gleason and Busch (2003), Masanes, Galley and Müller (2019), Sorkin (1994);
  • in the ABL rule of the two-state vector formalism, probability as squared amplitude is part of the rule;
  • Kastner and Cramer (2018) present a derivation of the Born square for radiative processes, in direct-action theory. They start from the requirement that a real photon involves both emission and absorption (their pp. 1 and 8-9). That the absorber's response carries the dual vector, and that the product is a probability, rests on the transactional picture (p. 2 and note 1). Both arguments are different from the copy counting of B-2.

No source derives that probabilities exist, or which outcome occurs. The data: three-path interference is zero within |κ| < 0.01 (Sinha and colleagues 2010) and κ̃₃ = 0.0 ± 3.1·10⁻⁵ in the classical regime of Kauten and colleagues (2017).

The protocol. Five formulations:

  • B-1: counting the paths without phases (a control);
  • B-2: counting copies of equal modulus (from premise U1);
  • B-3: number and mutual correlation (U2);
  • B-4: the loop (U3);
  • B-5: which norm the coin conserves.

With named assumptions:

  • H-es: probabilities exist and depend only on the state of the copies;
  • H-eq: copies of equal modulus are equally probable;
  • H-add: the probability of a set of distinct copies is the sum, normalised to 1;
  • H-cont: continuity for irrational ratios;
  • H-prod (B-4 only): the product of the forward and backward amplitudes is a probability.

The protocol was checked twice by a second AI system, with 9 and 4 remarks. All were accepted in two corrections registered before any calculation. There was no third round. The main points of the verifier became part of the classification: B-3 defines its measure as |Σw|², which is already the Born form; B-4 uses a quadratic rule as a probability; the complex conjugate of the backward amplitude follows from defining the return with the adjoint step.

4.7 The singlet curve −cos θ from the two ends of the nexus (new in 2.48.2)

The question mark in the title. It is the point of this section. In the translations that hold, the "mirror" between the two particles is a reading rule of a single nexus with two ends. It is not a signal that travels between the two ends. And the marginals stay zero: nothing can be sent with the mirror. So no "spooky action at a distance".

The question. Sections 4.1–4.6 put caps on the correlations: 2 for a common record read locally, 2√2 for Born readings at the two ends. But they never derive the curve that Bell experiments measure. It is the curve of two spin-½ particles in the singlet state: E(a, b) = −a·b = −cos θ. Here θ is the angle between the two measurement directions. With the Born rule, a two-dimensional complex state space and rotations that act as SU(2), the curve follows in one line. The question is: which of these ingredients can come from the two ends of the nexus, and which stay imported? There are five ingredients:

  • (i) the exponent 2;
  • (ii) an end as a two-state system with a phase;
  • (iii) the three directions of space as directions of the end, with the half angle;
  • (iv) the singlet as mirror reciprocity of the two ends;
  • (v) zero marginals, that is, no signal.

The method. Six translations, T0–T5, registered with the criteria C1–C5 before the program was run (13:24:31 UTC):

  • C1: E = −cos θ exactly, or within 10⁻³ on a grid;
  • C2: marginals within 10⁻³ of zero;
  • C3: S = 2√2 at the standard angles, and not above it on a grid of 64 directions;
  • C4: dependence on the relative angle only, in the plane and for 200 random pairs of directions in space;
  • C5: the list of what is imported.

The expected outcomes had been worked out by hand before the registration. It is a declared contaminated preregistration, not a blind prediction. The first registered version of the program stopped on a rounding error: a ratio of −1e-17 raised to the power ½. The corrected file, which keeps that ratio between 0 and 1, was registered before it was run (correction 1).

What the checks show.

  • T2, the mirror. With P(A = u) = ½ and the mirror, E = 2f(−c) − 1 holds whenever f(c) + f(−c) = 1 (symbolic calculation). The mirror gives the perfect anticorrelation at θ = 0: measuring in the same direction, the results are always opposite. And it gives the form of the correlation. But it does not give the curve. With a law linear in the angle it gives Bell's straight line and S = 2. In this model, once the mirror is given, going beyond 2 depends on the law with which one end is read. It is in line with section 4.1: the cap is set by the local reading.
  • T3, Wootters' route. With f = cos²g the Fisher information is 4g′². So "equal distinguishability for equal rotations" means g linear. Then f(0) = 1 and f(π) = 0 make κ a half-integer. Monotonicity selects κ = ½, that is, cos²(θ/2): the half angle, without putting SU(2) in by hand. If instead one counts the fractions without their noise, one gets the law linear in the angle (Bell's straight line). This is not new: Wootters (1980–81, not read directly here; known through Wootters 2013), Summhammer (quant-ph/9910039, equation 6), Brukner and Zeilinger (Found. Phys. 39, 677, 2009, with a quadratic measure of information assumed). It requires copies independent of each other. The author's premise of 13 October speaks instead of correlated copies.
  • T4, the light sides. The forms Q = Σ_{i<j} x_i x_j have eigenvalues (1, −½, −½) for three sides and (3/2, −½, −½, −½) for four: signatures (1, 2) and (1, 3). For null directions, that is, light directions, the normalised product gives sin²(θ/2) (deviation 4·10⁻¹⁶). Writing a light direction as 2ψψᵀ rotates ψ by θ/2. With three sides the readings lie in a plane. With four sides they cover the three directions, and T4b gives −a·b in space. The calculation uses only real directions. Complex numbers are not needed for these correlations, and the fourth side does not impose them. They enter only if one wants to write a light direction as the product of a two-component object (S² ≅ CP¹, the spinor–null-direction correspondence of Penrose and Rindler, quoted from memory, not checked here against the source). The pair rule is the Born rule of a qubit written on light directions: a translation by the AI, not new.
  • T1, the coin of 2.40. With χ ≠ π the correlation depends on the sum of the angles, and C4 fails. With k = 1 one gets −cos 2θ in the x–z plane, the photon curve, and isotropy in space breaks. With p ≠ 2, C1 fails, with S = 3.469182 (p = 3) and 3.771236 = 4·(2√2/3) (p = 4). These weights are not quantum mechanics. So this does not violate the quantum Tsirelson bound.
  • A registered expectation did not come true. For T4 with q = 2 (probability (η/η_opp)²) a failure of C1 was expected. Instead E = f(−c) − f(c) = −c exactly. But the marginal of B is (1 − c²)/2, up to 0.5 at θ = π/2. So C2 fails.
  • Compatibility with the experiments. No translation that holds deviates from −cos θ. It is a compatibility with the experiments, not a prediction. The values are read in the articles of the inventory (Sakai from the abstract only): Hensen and colleagues 2015, S = 2.42 ± 0.20; Storz and colleagues 2023, 2.0747 ± 0.0033; Poh and colleagues 2015, 2.82759 ± 0.00051; Sakai and colleagues 2006, proton pairs in the singlet, 2.83 ± 0.24 ± 0.07.

Reading for MM. The sentence of the common introduction on the mirror reciprocity of the two ends holds as a reason for the anticorrelation and for the form of the correlation. It does not hold for the curve: the curve comes from the law of one end. In every translation that holds, three ingredients are imported: the fair outcome of the first end read (v), the mirror as an update rule (iv), and the three directions as directions of the end, by isotropy or with the fourth side (iii).

5. Where MM stands among quantum interpretations

There is a classification called Local Friendliness. It says which assumptions must be given up by whoever accounts for quantum experiments. Cavalcanti and Wiseman (2021) define Local Agency like this: "the only relevant events correlated with an intervention are in its future light cone". Local Agency replaces Locality and No-Superdeterminism together. Then they split the relativistic causal arrow in two: the temporal causal arrow (causes come before effects) and relativistic causality (no influences faster than light).

7. What is not claimed, and what remains open

Not claimed:

  • a derivation of the Born rule without assumptions. In sections 4.1–4.5 quadratic weights, local Born responses and suitable measures over futures are assumed. Section 4.6 (2.48.1) obtains the Born rule from the counting of copies of equal modulus only with the declared assumptions H-es, H-eq and H-add (and H-cont for irrational ratios). The construction is similar to Zurek's, but H-eq is a declared premise. The physical necessity of H-eq is not derived;
  • a derivation of the Tsirelson bound beyond the published theorems;
  • a derivation of the singlet curve −cos θ without imported ingredients. In section 4.7 (2.48.2) the curve holds in three translations, each with the ingredients listed there: law of one end, mirror update, fair first outcome, three directions;
  • an experimental distinction from standard quantum mechanics;
  • mathematical priority.

What remains open:

  1. a rule that selects one outcome, with a measure over futures that gives Born. Section 4.5 shows that weights other than Born's make the consistent histories as improbable as one likes, in the pair model. To make this a reason for the Born weight, a postulate (written there) and a general argument are needed;
  2. why the copy that returns is the complex conjugate of the one that leaves. In the model it follows from defining the return with the adjoint step (section 4.6); a physical reason remains open. And why copies of equal modulus count the same (H-eq, which in section 4.6 is the author's premise U1);
  3. a locally mediated model beyond one singlet pair: more particles, networks, choices that adapt;
  4. why the hidden parameter is not accessible, and a causal argument for relativistic causality;
  5. the value of B(p) for 1 < p < 2, and its dependence on the dimension.

Each block runs in uscite/<block>/ and is compared with risultati/<block>/ according to the rules written in run_all.py:

  • standard output only, with the expected numpy division warnings removed from the references;
  • running times masked only where they are running times (end-of-line parentheses, "FINE in", "calcolate in"), plus the start dates and the numpy version;
  • decimal numbers equal within a relative tolerance of 10⁻⁹ plus an absolute one of 10⁻¹²; integers (and numbers written as integers in the logs), booleans, infinities and NaN exactly equal;
  • JSON compared without the time fields ("secondi", "sec"), right after each program and again at the end; figures not compared;
  • the exit code of each program stored; a missing output, a missing status or a non-zero exit is a failure;
  • controllo_d3.py checked as a matching prefix with exactly 8 starts and a final line.

"Matching" means in agreement with the stored references according to these rules. It does not certify the references themselves. Requirements: Python 3, numpy, scipy, matplotlib (tested with Python 3.13, numpy 2.4.6, scipy 1.18.0).

Glossary

  • Amplitude. The number, like an arrow with a length and a direction, that quantum mechanics gives to each outcome.
  • Born rule. The probability of an outcome is the square of the amplitude: |ψ|².
  • CHSH, score S. A number that measures how much the results of two distant sides agree: at most 2 with common instructions, at most 2√2 in quantum mechanics, at most 4 without signals.
  • Complex conjugate. The same complex number with the sign of the imaginary part changed.
  • Feynman checkerboard (MM 2.40). A model in which a particle moves forward in a zigzag; each turn has an imaginary weight.
  • Loop. The assumption W2: the outcome written in a record is selected by the future end of its history, which closes on itself.
  • Marginals. The averages of the results of one side only, looked at on its own. They are zero when every measurement of that side, on its own, gives +1 and −1 with the same frequency.
  • Non-local (in MM). Not located in spacetime, pregeometric. It does not mean an influence faster than light.
  • Registered. Written in a file and given a fingerprint before the test.
  • Retrocausal. A model in which a cause in the future has an effect in the past.
  • Self-consistency. The idea (Novikov) that only histories without contradictions can exist, even if the future acts on the past.
  • Signalling. Being able to send a message from one side to the other. In known physics it cannot be done faster than light.
  • Singlet. A state of two linked particles: measured in the same direction, their spins always give opposite results; their correlation is −cos θ.
  • Tsirelson bound. The maximum of S in quantum mechanics: 2√2, about 2.83.
  • TVD. Total variation distance between two probability distributions: 0 if equal, 1 if completely different.
